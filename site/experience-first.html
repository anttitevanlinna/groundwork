Experience before strategy

Acting creates the visceral proof whether or not something is real. You can read up on the internet all you like. You can follow the academia. You might develop anticipation. You don't develop data in your own domain and your own world with that.

The principle: you can't strategise a tool you've never touched — conviction arrives through your own hands on the work, never through a deck about it.

Before the strategy offsite, put your people in a room and let each build one real working thing with AI in an afternoon. The WOW creates demand no deck can — and because competence is the ceiling every later platform, governance and team-scale move is bounded by, this is the Intent-phase company move the rest of the transformation rests on. Strategy is the frame; experience is the fuel. If you must sequence them, fuel first.

Deck → nodding. Hands → spreading. You can't sell a future they've never touched.
The reflex that fails · why this transformation is different

Every past transformation rewarded the deck. This one punishes it.

Digital, agile, cloud — every transformation you've run before was vision-led, and rightly so. A CEO could picture a "digital company" through pure analogy, never having written a line of code, and the picture was close enough to align around. So the reflex is hardwired: kick off with a vision, build the strategy, pick the tools, drive adoption. That order has worked your whole career. The trouble is that agents quietly broke the first link in the chain.

Say the words "an agent that autonomously processes invoices across three systems" to someone who has never run one, and you get a polite nod over an empty space. The gap isn't knowledge a slide could close — it's experience, and no deck bridges it. So the honest diagnosis after most AI kickoffs is the most damning one a leader can write down: they nodded, and nothing changed. The reflex didn't misfire; the reflex was the problem. The fix is to run the same four pieces in reverse — the diagram below puts the two orders side by side.

Safety as the floor under the sequence is well-supported as a direction: people won't risk a first attempt where a failed experiment gets them blamed. Treat the direction as solid; treat the percentages as one survey, not an established norm.

Here's the part that stings a builder-leader most, because it demotes the move you're best at. Standing up and telling people AI matters — the thing every change-management playbook tells you to do — has roughly zero direct effect on whether they adopt it. What moves adoption is a colleague saying, in their own words, how AI changed their Tuesday. Your encouragement isn't worthless; it's just indirect. You don't push the change through the org. You set the conditions under which peers carry it for you. The diagram below draws the two leader-shapes: the single thick arrow that bounces off, and the small forum the change spreads out of.

You don't drive AI adoption. You create the conditions for peers to drive it — and then get out of the thread's way.

The dose · how much hands-on it actually takes

A demo they watch doesn't take. A thing they build does.

The WOW is not a polished demo on a big screen — watching someone else's magic produces applause, not pull. It's the person's own hands on the keyboard, building one small real thing, hitting the moment where the output is genuinely theirs. And it takes a meaningful dose to get there: below some floor people poke at it once, get a mediocre result, and quietly conclude AI "isn't there yet." Above the floor they tip into regular use. In-person beats remote on every measure, because the WOW needs shared air and a colleague leaning over to say "wait, show me how you did that."

How big is "meaningful"? A useful order-of-magnitude exists, but be careful how you carry it. A BCG consultancy-survey finding (directional, Jun 2025) reports 79% of employees with five-plus hours of hands-on time became regular users, versus 67% below — and that only about a third feel properly trained. Take the shape of that: a real dose, not a lunch-and-learn. Do not turn it into a law that "people need five hours" — your threshold depends on your people and your tools. The Mollick MBA experiment points the same direction by a different route: a compelling single experiment (One Useful Thing, Jan 2026) in which non-engineers built working startup prototypes in four days, and the management skills they already had — delegating, judging output, explaining intent — turned out to be the exact AI skills. One cohort, one instructor, unreplicated; persuasive, not proven.

The five-hour number · directional, not a dose law

BCG, Jun 2025 — vendor / consultancy survey, treat as directional. 79% of employees with 5+ hours hands-on became regular users vs 67% below; in-person beat online; only ~33% said they were properly trained. Useful as an order-of-magnitude — "give it a real dose, in person" — not as a proven universal dose. "People need five hours" is a coronation the data doesn't earn; your number depends on your people and tools.

The WOW moment isn't educational — it's strategic in the way that it changes the pace of learning. Chatting and following the media does not teach you enough of what is really possible. Just like watching football does not teach you to play football. To learn to play, you have to play.

“Skills-First AI Transformation Strategy” · Apr 2026 · he played football through his youth

Then the interesting thing happens, and it's the whole reason to start here. People who've built one working thing stop waiting to be told where AI fits. They go find their own high-value process — the report nobody enjoys, the reconciliation that eats a Thursday — because competence creates pull (this shows up in the field, e.g. a Nordic security-software org, where adoption stopped needing a champion once people had the skill). A person who has built a piece of it can finally imagine their own role redesigned around it, which is the muscle that separates AI high-performers from dabblers. The loop below is the engine: build · tell a peer · peer wants in · joins the next session · builds · tells a peer. Each turn the circle of competent builders widens — and strategy rides on top of it, written second.

Use when

You're about to kick off the AI transformation and your instinct is a strategy deck, a roadmap, a platform shortlist — or a skeptic won't move on your slides, or adoption stalled after a tools rollout that nobody actually changed behaviour around.

Build

A WOW session where each person built one real thing — plus a standing peer forum: voluntary, recurring, no mandate. The strategy comes second.

Test

People build unprompted, and a skeptic flips on proof they made with their own hands.
Not merely "96% adopted the tool."

The forum is the engine, not the speech: compound interest on a WOW. Each pass widens the circle of people who can imagine their own role redesigned — and the strategy you write later has competent people to run it.

Monday move: cancel the offsite, book a room. Five willing people, real laptops, each builds one small real thing by the end — no slides, no roadmap, no goalposts. A calendar invite · a room · two hours. Then ask one question — "what would you build next?" — and write down what they say. That list is your strategy input, and they wrote it, not you.

Test · how you know it ran, not just played

The signal that it took is people building unprompted — after a couple of hands-on sessions they start without being told where to look. A skeptic — the rational professional, not a technophobe — flips when they see role-specific proof they made themselves, never from another deck. And the WOW shows up second-hand: a colleague says, in their own words, how watching a coworker use AI changed their mind. The negative test is the tools-first trap dressed as success — high adoption, no behaviour change (96% adopt, 4% see ROI). If the numbers look great and Tuesday looks identical, you shipped tools, not the WOW.

Why
The principle

Competence is the ceiling, not capability. Strategy without competent people is governance of an abstraction.

You can't envision what you've never experienced — so stop selling the future and let them build a piece of it.

The deck doesn't land because it can't: the perception gap (76% of executives believe staff are enthusiastic; 31% are) means leaders are reasoning about a room that isn't there. The mechanism underneath is mental-model formation — a person who has built one working thing can finally picture their own role redesigned around AI, and the people who redesign their workflow are the ones who become high performers (a McKinsey consultancy survey, directional, puts them 2.8x more likely). You cannot deck your way to that mental model. You have to hand them the keyboard.

The counter-evidence is real and resolves cleanly. Deloitte (consultancy, directional) finds formal-strategy orgs report far higher implementation success — but strategy and experience are complementary, not competing: strategy is the frame, experience is the fuel. Shopify's AI mandate worked because the CEO already had the WOW in his own hands; the mandates that failed came from leaders who hadn't. So frame, then fuel — and if forced to sequence, fuel first. Note the worst-case path too: a mandate without the experience underneath buys you compliance and roughly 2.2x the resistance.

Frame, then fuel. If forced to sequence — fuel first.

Once someone builds something real with an agent, the vision forms itself. No slide deck does what 2 hours of hands-on experience does. The sequence is: experience first, vision second. Always.
