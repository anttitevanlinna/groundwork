Read the room before you run

The principle: Context is King — and an unexamined window quietly biases every move after it.

Before you paste the task, make the agent's read of the repo an inspected fact, not a fluent assumption: ask for a deliberate read, interrogate what it skipped, quote one file back, and check the budget you have left. A checked read is the precondition for evidence worth compounding into the shared kit — the whole org's learning loop inherits the quality of its orientation habit.

The summary sounded oriented. Orientation is what you checked — the skip list, one quote-back, and the budget.
The mechanism · why fluency fools you

The agent's confidence tracks its prose, not its accuracy.

Here is the trap, and it is a good one. When you open a session and ask the agent to look at the repo, it reports back fluently, specifically, reassuringly — and it does this whether or not it read the right files. The prose is calibrated; the read may not be. That is the design, not a bug: you prime it, the LLM scales whatever you primed. A vague prime — "have a look at the repo" — gets scaled into a vague, shallow, partly-reconstructed read, delivered with the same poise as a careful one. Fluency is not evidence. The cost of mistaking one for the other doesn't show up now; it shows up three steps later as a fix aimed at the wrong file.

The fork is the prime, and the diagram below draws it. Send the agent in vague and it scales your vagueness: it names what it happened to glance at and stops. Send it in deliberate — "read what's load-bearing, skip what isn't worth it, then tell me what you skipped and why" — and you get an inspected read with its own seams showing. Same repo, same model, two completely different substrates for everything downstream. The whole operating model rides on whichever one you produced in the first five minutes.

Process diagram · 01Same repo, same model. The prime is the fork — a vague look scales to a shallow read; a deliberate one scales to an inspected read with its own skip list showing.

The deliberate prime gets you a better read — but a better read is still a claim, not ground truth. The agent's account of what it read and why is a reconstruction, and reconstructions confabulate: it will sometimes give you a tidy reason for a file it never actually opened. So the second move is to stop nodding and start interrogating. Ask the one question almost nobody asks — "what did you skip, and why?" The skip list is where the risk lives, and the agent rarely volunteers it. Then spot-check: quote one specific file or function back and ask what's actually in it. Claims aren't evidence until you've checked one.

The agent's self-report is a hypothesis, not ground truth. Treat it like one.

The upgrade · from summary you nod at to read you checked

Turn the read from a one-way summary into a checked artefact.

The upgrade is cheap and it changes the epistemics entirely. A one-way summary asks for your trust; a checked read earns it. You surface the read-vs-skipped split, you quote-check one claim against the actual file, and you run /context to see the budget — the number and the breakdown across system prompt, messages, memory and skills. The slice the agent didn't load is the bounded-window reality you're now steering inside. Below: the summary on the left that you'd otherwise nod at, and the same read on the right after the arrow tagged "fluency → evidence."

Process diagram · 02Fluency → evidence. The read becomes a checked artefact — skip list surfaced, one claim quote-checked, the budget read. Assume a working ~10% of what the agent says is misrepresentation until you've checked one — a curriculum heuristic, could be more or less; the point is to check, not to trust the number.
Working heuristic

The "~10% misrepresentation" figure is a curriculum-stated working assumption, not a measured rate — could be more, could be less. Its job is to make you spot-check one claim, not to be reasoned from as if it were a finding. Wherever the number appears on this page, that caveat travels with it.

Orientation isn't a one-shot ritual you perform at session start and forget. It's a loop, and it tightens. Each pass you orient, you ask what got skipped, you quote-check a claim, you read /context — and then you spend the next round's bytes more deliberately because you now know which ones were wasted last time. The unread slice shrinks toward the part that genuinely doesn't matter. The session that produces a clean, checked read is also the one whose evidence the downstream compound step can actually trust and encode. Garbage orientation in, garbage to compound from. A checked read out, something worth keeping.

The compounding loop · what changes between passes

Each pass, the unread slice shrinks toward what doesn't matter.

The picture below draws the four beats with the window's unread wedge getting visibly thinner across passes. Notice it never closes to zero — orientation isn't about reading everything, which would just burn the budget you came to protect. It's about driving the unread slice down to the part you've consciously decided is safe to leave out. That conscious leaving-out is the skill. By the third pass you're not reading more; you're reading righter, and you can say exactly what's not in the window and why that's fine.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

You're not testing the summary. You're testing whether you checked.

The honest test is uncomfortable, because it indicts the most natural thing to do. If you read the agent's summary, nodded, and moved straight to the fix — you skipped the move, however oriented the summary sounded. So the test is falsifiable and it lives in the scrollback: can you name one file the agent chose not to read, and say whether that matches what you'd have expected? Did you quote one file back and watch the claim hold — or catch it where it didn't? Can you point at the /context percentage and say which slice of the repo is not in the window right now? Three yeses and you oriented. Any no and you're steering blind in fluent prose. That last one — catching the fluent-but-wrong — is the foundation skill everything downstream leans on: the plan you'll pressure-test, the test you'll write first, the evidence you'll compound. Skip orientation and all of it inherits an unchecked map.

Use when

You're about to set an agent loose in a repo and you haven't checked what it can actually see — every session start, every switch of repo or feature, and every time a session goes sideways and you realise it was working from a map you never checked.

Build

A checked orientation — not a document you keep, a state you reach: the read, its read-vs-skipped account, one confirmed quote-back, and a /context reading. The map is inspected, not assumed.

Test

You can name one file it didn't read, one claim you quote-checked, and the unread slice.
Not merely "the summary read well."

Why
The principle

Context is King. You prime, the LLM scales — including your vagueness.

"The agent's self-report is a hypothesis, not ground truth."

The counterintuitive part is that the agent's confidence is calibrated to its prose, not its accuracy. It flatters, it progresses, it mirrors your stance — reporting the repo fluently whether or not it read the right files. Two mechanisms ride underneath. First, context is whatever you tell it: the window colours everything downstream, so an unexamined window quietly biases every later move — the wrong feature read becomes the wrong fix, three steps on. Second, the window has a ceiling: every byte spent on noise is a byte not available for the work, so seeing the budget is what lets you spend it on purpose.

Orientation is how you make the prime deliberate — and how you check the right context actually loaded. Skipping it doesn't save time; it moves the cost downstream, where it's more expensive to find. A checked read is the substrate the plan, the failing test, and the compounded evidence all ride on. Skip this and the rest is built on a map you never looked at.

You can't steer what you can't see.
Test · the negative case

The fastest tell you skipped the move: the scrollback shows no /context in the first ten minutes, no introspection prompt after the read, and no quote-back when something felt off. A clean orientation leaves all three as deliberate moves in the transcript — "you said you read X, what's in it?" lands before the first edit, not after the wrong one.
