Orientation

You do not know what you don't know when working in unfamiliar domains. You don't know the effective way to work in that domain either. But you can try.

The principle: you can't tell, from a fluent summary, what the agent never read — and the unread slice quietly biases every move after it.

Think of /context as a land survey you run before breaking ground: read what the agent actually loaded, where its read thins out, and how much budget is left — so the honest deliverable can be I don't yet know enough to touch this file. A checked read is the precondition for evidence worth compounding into the shared kit — the whole org's learning loop inherits the quality of its orientation habit.

Beat one · the survey is the move

A fluent summary is a confident map of ground nobody walked.

Borrow the surveyor's discipline for exactly one sentence: you don't pour a foundation on a parcel you haven't walked, even when the drawing looks finished. Now drop the metaphor, because the rest is plain agent mechanics. When you open a session and say "have a look at the repo," the agent reports back fluently, specifically, reassuringly — and it does this whether or not it read the right files. The prose is calibrated to your prime, not to the ground. You prime, the model scales whatever you primed; a vague look gets scaled into a shallow, partly-reconstructed read delivered with the poise of a careful one. The cost doesn't show up now. It shows up three steps later as a fix aimed at the wrong file.

So run the survey deliberately. Ask for a read that names what's load-bearing and skips what isn't worth it, then says what it skipped and why — you're recruiting its judgment, not its completeness. Then stop nodding and interrogate, because a better read is still a claim: ask "what did you skip, and why?" — the skip list is where the risk hides and the agent rarely volunteers it — and quote one file back, "you said you read auth.go — what's actually in it?", to watch the claim hold or catch it where it doesn't. Then run /context and read the budget: the number and the breakdown across system prompt, messages, memory and skills. The slice that didn't load is the bounded-window reality you're now steering inside.

Beat two · the honest deliverable
Survey verdict · the frontier output

Sometimes the survey comes back and the only honest call is: I don't yet know enough to touch this file.

That is not a failed session — it is the deliverable. The unread auth.go wasn't surveyed, the quote-back didn't hold, the budget says the load-bearing slice never entered the window. A consultancy demo would push past that and ship the confident fix anyway. The move is to refuse — to name the unknown as the result and spend the next round's bytes closing it, rather than paper over it with fluent prose.

Did you read deliberately?

Load-bearing named, rest skipped on purpose, with reasons.

Can you name a skip?

One file it chose not to read — and whether that surprises you.

Did one claim hold?

Quote a file back; watch it hold or catch it confabulating.

What's the unread slice?

Point at the /context percentage not in the window right now.

Working heuristic

The curriculum's working assumption is that roughly ~10% of what the agent says is misrepresentation until you've checked — a stated heuristic, not a measured rate, could be more or less. Its only job is to make you spot-check one claim, not to be reasoned from as a finding.

Do it once and it stops being a ritual and becomes a reflex: orient, ask what got skipped, quote-check, read /context — then spend the next round's bytes where the last round wasted them. The unsurveyed slice shrinks toward the part that genuinely doesn't matter; it never closes to zero, and the skill is knowing exactly what you've decided is safe to leave out of the window and why. Monday move: before you paste the next task, slow down for five minutes and run those four — a repo you already have open, /context built in, before a line of code moves. The session that lands a clean, checked survey is the one whose evidence everything downstream — the plan, the test you write first, the rule you compound — can actually trust.

The cheapest survey stake you can drive is a sentence: I don't know enough yet to touch this. The agent will never say it for you. Saying it first is the orientation.
