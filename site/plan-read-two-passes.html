Two reads, one approval

The principle: one kind of scrutiny catches one kind of miss — a human read and an agent's branch-walk catch different things.

Before you approve an agent's plan for a multi-file change, give it two different kinds of scrutiny — your situated read for the soft item only you can feel, and a second agent's branch-walk for everything you'd never hold in working memory — because neither read alone is complete.

The plan didn't get better because you stared harder. It got read twice, two different ways.
The mechanism · why one read isn't enough

Two reads, two different misses.

Start with the honest tell, because everyone recognises it: when was the last time an agent wrote a plan you approved without really reading? We all do it. The plan touches three files, lists verification steps, reads like a decision — and the approve button is right there while you're on a tight slot. The trap isn't laziness, it's structure. A seven-item plan with headers looks like a decision even when it's still a draft. Structure is persuasive; that's exactly what makes it dangerous at the approval prompt.

The fix isn't reading harder. It's reading twice, two different ways, because a single pass can only ever catch one shape of mistake. Your read is shallow but situated — you don't hold the whole design tree in your head, but you can feel the soft item, the assumption that's wrong for this repo, the file that shouldn't be touched at all. A second agent's walk-down is the inverse: exhaustive but unsituated — it surfaces every unresolved branch in the design, including the dozen you'd never have kept in working memory, but it has no nose for which one would quietly break your codebase. Process diagram · 01Two reads, drawn as two catch-fields. They overlap on the obvious; the gap on each side is what the other read alone would have missed.

The non-overlap is the whole reason you run both. Your read carries voice-of-experience the agent's breadth can't fake: you've seen what happens when the auth helper gets edited for a feature that had nothing to do with auth. The agent has never shipped your code at 5pm on a Friday. The walk-down, meanwhile, carries a completeness you can't match by squinting — it will dredge up the nullable-id question, the cache-or-live question, the retry-path question, one at a time, until it runs out of branches. Pair the two and you get a complete read. Run either alone and you've shipped a draft you mistook for a decision.

Assumption-silent isn't assumption-free. Every plan assumes something; the good ones say what.

The upgrade · rubber-stamp → paired read

From the sixty-second nod to a gate the plan has to cross.

The default move is a rubber-stamp: a plan approved in under a minute on a single glance, where the structure did the persuading and your read added nothing. The upgrade is the same approval prompt run as a deliberate two-pass gate. First, push back with what only you can see — and do it specifically. Pick keep planning with feedback, find one step, and quote its number and a word from it: not "looks good, one concern," but "step 3 — 'update the auth helper' — that's the wrong file for this change." A quote forces the agent to revise the actual step instead of nodding at a vague worry. Then run the second read. Below: the nod on the left, the gate on the right.

Process diagram · 02The approval is the same button. What changed is the gate the plan crosses to reach it — a quoted push-back, then a branch-walk run to exhaustion.

Two things break the gate if you skip them. The first: the agent will say "good point" and re-soften the same step in the revised plan. An acknowledgement is not a fix. Check the regenerated step — if it re-softened, push again, same quote. The second: run the walk-down as a Q-and-A sequence, not a one-shot dump. Ask the agent to walk the design tree it didn't resolve, branch by branch, one question with a recommended answer each, and let it run until it's out of branches — you don't get to decide it's finished early. A prompt that returns every branch in one list is a list; a sequence that resolves them one at a time is a read.

The compounding loop · the plan-reading gets cheaper

Each pass pre-shapes the next plan in this repo.

Here's the counterintuitive part, and it's also where the move stops being a chore. This feels expensive — two reads on every plan? — but it's cheaper than it looks, for two reasons. One: you don't have to execute a plan to know it's good. Making the plan good is the work; the running is just typing. Two: the branch that sharpened how plans get made here doesn't evaporate when you approve. The same wrong-for-this-repo assumption your push-back caught becomes a saved rule, and the next plan in this repo arrives pre-shaped by it — fewer soft items, fewer unresolved branches, less pushing-back to land. What "Test" actually means · the negative check

If you can't name what your read caught, the two reads collapsed into one.

The clean test isn't "did I approve a plan." It's "can I prove it got two distinct kinds of scrutiny." The scrollback should show a keep-planning message quoting a specific step number and a word from it — not "looks good, one concern." It should show a Q-and-A branch-walk that ran to exhaustion, not a single dumped list. At least one walk-down recommendation should have been rejected and corrected for this codebase — the recommendations are defaults, not prescriptions. And the honest negative test: name one thing your push-back caught that the agent's walk-down would have missed. If you can't, the two reads collapsed into one — the agent did both passes and your situated read added nothing. That's the failure mode hiding behind a clean approval.

Use when

An agent hands you a clean-looking plan for a multi-file change and the approve button is right there — the work is non-trivial enough that touching the wrong file matters, and the plan's confidence outruns your actual read of it.

Build

An approved plan file that carries both reads in its history — the gates you read, the soft items your push-back hardened, the branches the walk-down resolved. Note the path: it's the input to the next phase.

Core moves

The action, in sequence

Each move is built to catch a different kind of miss.
1

Read it as three artefacts

File list, verification steps, named assumptions — separately, not one wall.

2

Push back with a quote

Quote a step number and a word. The soft item only you can see.

3

Check it sharpened

Don't accept a nod. If it re-softened, push again.

4

Walk down the branches

One question at a time, to exhaustion. You don't call it done.

5

Reject one, then approve

Defaults, not prescriptions. Then stop — you don't execute.

read→push back→walk down→approve→save the branch as a rule

Monday move: next plan an agent hands you, don't approve it. Keep planning with feedback · quote one step + one word · then walk the branches one at a time · reject one · approve. No platform, no budget, no sign-off — push-back is a chat message, the walk-down is a prompt, all inside one session on a task already in your backlog.

Why
The principle

One kind of scrutiny catches one kind of miss. Pair two, and the gap between them is where the plan-reading skill lives.

"Plan mode makes the read possible; it isn't the read."

The mechanism is plain once you name it. Your read is shallow but situated — you feel the soft item, the assumption that's wrong for this repo, the file that shouldn't be touched. The agent's walk-down is exhaustive but unsituated — it surfaces every branch in the design tree, including the ones you'd never have held in working memory. Reading a plan is finite; pair the two and you get a complete read. Neither alone does.

And it's cheaper than it feels, because you don't have to execute a plan to know it's good — the approval is the work, not the running. Assumption-silent isn't assumption-free. Every plan assumes something, and the good ones say what; the two reads are how the silent assumptions get named before they cost you a file.

The structure persuaded you. Two reads is how you stop letting it.
Where it sits · personal → team → company

This is the personal foundation of Context & Guardrails — the read-discipline an individual practises at the approval prompt. It doesn't stay personal. The same branch a second read surfaces is what a team later hardens into a shared judge or a CI check that catches it before any teammate has to ask — so that the company's plan-quality stops depending on whether each individual happened to read twice. The habit you build on one plan is the seed of a gate the whole org rests on. (Positioning, not a coined law.)
