Train before you buy

The principle: clarity is the ceiling, not capability — you cannot design an organisation for a capability nobody in it understands.

Put the platform decision last, not first. Build a little competence inside the building before any vendor is in your calendar, and the unanswerable board question — "which platform?" — decomposes into three small, reversible ones. The repo's strategic frame, in order: competence → discovery → context → platform.

Same fork, two orders. On the dead path the platform is the first node; on the living one it's the last.
The mechanism · why the question is unanswerable first

Same board question, different answer — once competence exists.

Picture the room. Four horizontal platforms, five-plus vertical SaaS vendors, an open-source framework someone keeps championing, and roughly two hundred internal processes that might one day be agents. The board wants a recommendation by quarter-end. Every demo looks plausible; every evaluation is quietly shaped by whichever vendor presented last. The honest trouble isn't which platform — it's that nobody in the room can yet formulate the right question, because the right question only arrives once someone has actually built. The mental model is the missing input, and no slide supplies it.

This is structurally new. Past transformations — digital, agile, cloud — could be vision-led because the concepts were reachable by analogy: a CEO never wrote code, yet could picture a digital company. Agents break that. Say "an agent that processes invoices across three systems with human escalation at decision points" to someone who's never built or used one, and they hear "automation" and quietly file it under RPA. The gap isn't knowledge — it's experience, and you cannot brief your way across it. So the first move isn't a decision at all. It's a refusal: don't choose the platform yet.

The hardest problem isn't choosing a platform — it's knowing what questions to ask. Skip to platform selection and you're choosing between vendor narratives.

Process diagram · 01The big unanswerable question doesn't get answered — it gets decomposed. Competence is the only input that changed.

The traditional sequence runs assess → select platform → big procurement → 6-month pilot → maybe rollout. It feels responsible. It produces PowerPoints and vendor lock-in. The competence-first sequence inverts it: train people to build agents first, start with coding agents — the cheap, low-risk meta-platform — and let competence create the pull. People then discover their own high-value processes, and the infrastructure questions arrive scoped and answerable instead of abstract and board-sized. The consultancy default (assess → identify → build → train) is exactly backwards; competence-first flips it to train → identify → build → assess.

Notice the order has no shortcut around step one. One person builds something for themselves and gets the mental model. Personal use is the floor, not a warm-up — skip it and nothing above holds: no shared vision, no organisational learning, just a tidy procurement plan resting on an abstraction. And the people who've built are also your best surveyors. Builders find the gaps faster and more accurately than any consultancy assessment, and the gaps are predictable: three walls, every time — data access, somewhere to run what they build, and discoverability so others can find and reuse it. Those three are the real questions. Each is a lightweight two-way door.

The upgrade move · vendor narrative → builder evidence

From a procurement funnel to a pull engine.

The diagram below redraws the funnel as an engine. Same components, reversed wiring. The old funnel pours effort in at the top — assess, select, procure, pilot — and the platform decision sits at the very front, made by people who have watched demos. The pull engine starts with a tiny rep (train → build), lets discovery do the surveying, and defers the platform call to the very end, where it's now small, reversible, and run against your use cases rather than the vendor's reel. The person who's built one agent walks into the procurement meeting with evidence; everyone else walks in with narratives.

Process diagram · 02Same five stages, reversed wiring. Move the platform node from the front to the back and PowerPoints become questions worth answering.

Here's the part that makes it a foundation rather than a tactic. Competence doesn't just answer the platform question — it starts a chain. Competence creates mental models → mental models create shared vision → shared vision connects experiments → connected experiments become organisational learning → which pulls more people in to build. Each pass, the soil gets richer: a common language emerges, isolated wins start referencing each other, and the organisation can finally hold a coherent picture of what it's building toward. Skip the first link and the chain never forms — you get the HBR experimentation trap: hundreds of pilots that never connect, never scale, never compound.

You cannot design an organisation for a capability nobody in it understands. Build competence and the platform question shrinks from a board decision to a two-way door.

The evidence · convergence, not proof

What the research actually says — caveats attached.

This is a convergence read, not a law. Several independent signals point the same way; each carries a caveat that must travel with it, because a number stripped of its genre is just a louder opinion. Read them as directional pressure, not measurement. The qualitative spine — the sequence, the mechanism, the field observation — is sturdier than any single statistic.

Use when

A vendor is in your calendar before anyone in the building has built an agent — a procurement cycle opening on zero internal competence, leadership "excited about AI," or an isolated experiment that "worked" with nothing connecting it to the next one.

Build

Not a platform pick. A competence base plus a scoped, evidence-backed shortlist of the three real infrastructure questions — produced by people who've built, not watched demos.

Test

Domain experts name the candidates and "people build unprompted."
Not "we signed a platform contract."

Why
The principle

Clarity is the ceiling, not capability. The bottleneck is organisational, not technical.

"You cannot design an organisation for a capability nobody in it understands."

Without competence there is no vision — only governance of an abstraction. The chain is plain: competence → mental models → shared vision → connected experiments → organisational learning. Skip the first link and the chain never forms; you get the HBR experimentation trap — hundreds of isolated pilots that never scale. The whole point of training before buying is that the first link is the only one you can't import, brief, or procure.

And the timing argument is just as sharp: nobody should be making non-two-way-door decisions on the agent landscape right now — the space is pre-chasm and every platform is immature. Competence-first defers exactly the expensive, irreversible calls and pulls forward the cheap, reversible ones. The person who's built one agent walks into the procurement meeting with evidence. Everyone else walks in with vendor narratives.

Train before you buy — the platform question shrinks to a two-way door.
How you know it's real

The tell isn't a signed contract — it's behaviour. People build unprompted: nobody was assigned "build an agent for X," the competence itself created the pull. (a Nordic security-software org field observation, March 2026 — practitioner-direct, Level 2: after two modules of training, pretty much everyone started building dashboards, agents, applications.)

The questions get smaller and more specific — from "which platform should we standardise on?" to "where does this app live, and how does my colleague find it?" Vague-and-vendor-shaped giving way to scoped-and-reversible is the signal. And the candidates get named by domain experts, not consultants — who are right more often, because they know the work. The negative test: if the only "competence" in the building is a signed contract and a training plan to follow it, the sequence ran backwards.
