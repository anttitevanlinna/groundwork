Share a slice that absorbs

The principle: Access is not absorption — the gap between rolling it out and having it actually used is the whole problem in one phrase.

Your personal agent works because of a thousand choices you never wrote down. You can't hand those over with a folder share. This pattern shows which slice to offer, and how to design the handoff so calibration actually transfers.

Four surfaces to share. One shape to never reach for. The choice between them is the design decision.
The mechanism · what you can't hand over with a folder share

The agent works because of things you never wrote down.

The CTO who built a compounding research agent has an invisible co-pilot: a thousand calibration decisions baked into every click she makes. Which source to trust when two contradict each other. What "board-ready" means to her board specifically. Which output sentence smells like hallucination. None of that is in any file. It's in her, accumulated over hundreds of sessions where she ran the agent, noticed something wrong, and pushed back. That's calibration. And it cannot be exported.

The failure mode is elegant in its predictability: share the whole system, and her head of strategy either trusts it completely — no pushback, no critical reading, the wrong outputs get used — or trusts it not at all and goes back to spreadsheets. The calibrated middle ground she's earned takes about six months of use to build. You can't hand it over. You can only design the conditions for someone else to build their own. That's the job of this pattern.

Interview first · the job to be done comes before the surface

Name the teammate. Then ask what job they're trying to get done.

The most common sharing mistake is solving for your candidate before understanding their job. You've built a research agent. Your head of strategy could use it. So you show it to her. But she's already hired something for that job — probably a combination of a trusted analyst, a Slack channel, and morning coffee with the right person. Every job already has a current hire. The question isn't "will she use it?" but "what would have to be true for her to fire the incumbent?" That answer is social nine times out of ten.

The interview has three outcome vectors, and the non-obvious one is the one that earns its keep. Speed and quality are obvious axes — you expected those. The third vector is what the conversation surfaces: a dependency removed, an anxiety reduced, a scope expanded. The third outcome vector is where the actual job lives, and it's never the one the teammate names up front. Write it down. Then pick the surface that moves that specific job. You're now designing for their work, not your assumption of their work.

The teammate's job comes first; your candidate comes second. In that order. The move only works at one-to-one granularity first.

The people plan · what PowerPoints omit

The technical plan fills in quickly. The people plan is where it stalls.

Every agent-sharing project I've seen gets to a working technical plan within a week. The artefact, the source files, the rules, the delivery cadence — those questions answer themselves fast. The people plan stalls. Not because it's hard, but because it requires naming names, and naming names surfaces politics. Who owns this when you're on holiday? Who trains the next person? What happens when the owner leaves the company? A perfect technical plan with no people plan is a PowerPoint. It ships. Nothing changes.

The people plan has five columns: owner, governance, operating, accountability, propagation. They weigh equal to the technical plan — equal thickness, equal priority, equal visibility in the deck you present. The absorption bottleneck isn't a technology problem. Research from CircleCI's 2026 report across 28 million+ workflow runs found roughly 19 in 20 teams fail to scale generation into delivery — the gap is always organisational, not computational. The same pattern holds in business-process sharing: the slice gets built, then sits unused because nobody owns the absorption side.

What "test" actually means · the crossing signal

The test that matters is them using it without you present.

The demo is not the crossing. The first time she pulls it up without you in the room — that's the signal. Better still: she corrects an output. Not accepts it wholesale, not ignores it — she reads it, finds something wrong with it, and pushes back. Calibration is accumulating. The highest-quality signal is when she names a limitation unprompted: "it's good for X, but I don't use it for Y." That's not scepticism. That's calibrated use. The failure state is the inverse: either using it for everything (zero calibration) or nothing (wall-crossing failed). Both look similar from outside.

Use when

A system that works for you might lift someone else — but you don't know yet which slice of it actually travels. You're tempted to share the whole thing. Resist. This is the pattern for the first crossing.

Build

One shareable artefact sized to the surface chosen — context folder, skill file, pushed output, or queryable interface — plus a one-page absorption plan naming the owner, the review rhythm, and the first failure mode the teammate should expect.

Test

The teammate uses it without you present.
Better: they correct an output.
Best: they name a limitation.
Fail: the demo you arranged.

Core moves

The action, in sequence

One person, one surface, one absorption plan — in that order.
1

Name one person

One teammate, not "the team." The move only works at one-to-one first.

2

Interview for the job

Incumbent? Metric? Third outcome vector? Text them the hypothesis and ask if it's right.

3

Choose the smallest surface

Context · skill · output · interface. The choice between them is the design decision.

4

Write the people plan

Owner, governance, operating, accountability, propagation. Weighs equal to the technical plan.

5

Watch for calibration

Independent use. A correction. A named limitation. That's the crossing confirmed.

name the teammate→interview for the job→pick the surface→people plan→watch for calibration

Then repeat for the next person. Each crossing is a vote on which surface shape your organisation can actually absorb — that pattern IS your company's adoption strategy, whether you called it that or not.

Monday move: Pick the one person who came to mind reading the use-when. Before tomorrow: write one sentence — "what job are they trying to get done this week?" — not what they'd use your agent for, but what outcome they're actually chasing. Text it to them and ask if it's right. No platform · no budget · no sign-off. A text message and ten minutes is the whole first move.

Why
The principle

Access is not absorption. Calibration cannot be handed over — only the conditions for building it can.

"Access gives them the artifact. Calibration only comes from breaking the thing yourself."

There is a wide gap between the number of organisations that have rolled out AI access and the number where it has reached genuine production trust: broad access, rare production readiness. The same structure shows up across domains — legal, finance, security, enterprise — widely enough that the pattern is not in doubt, even though we won't put a survey-grade number on the spread we can't yet stand behind. The bottleneck is organisational, not computational. This card is the mechanism for crossing it — one person, one slice, one absorption plan at a time.

Polanyi's principle explains why the whole-agent share always fails: "we know more than we can tell." The CTO's calibration is tacit. It cannot be documented or transferred as content. But it CAN be replicated — not by handing over the 800 hours, but by designing a surface small enough that the teammate builds their own 800 hours quickly. A context share with a tight feedback loop will get them there faster than a full system share that overwhelms them on day one.

Absorption-first sharing: not "what can I hand over?" but "what can they actually absorb this week?" The systemic line: this card IS the wall-crossing — the specific mechanism by which a personal agent system begins to compound at team or company scope. Without it, every other pattern in the personal stack stays privately held.

You can't hand over calibration. You can only create the conditions for someone else to build their own.
Test · in practice

The negative test is blunt: if the only use you observe is during a demo you arranged, the technical plan shipped and the absorption plan didn't. Set up no more demos. Watch what happens next Monday without you in the room. If nothing happens, the sharing surface was wrong — or the people plan is missing a name.

An independent correction is worth more than ten successful demo uses. It means calibration is accumulating. A named limitation — "it's good for X but not Y" — is the highest signal. That's scoped trust. That's the wall crossed.
