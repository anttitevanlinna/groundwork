Sharing an agent

The principle: access is not absorption — the gap between rolling it out and having it actually used is the whole problem in one phrase.

Your agent runs on a thousand calibration calls you never wrote into CLAUDE.md — which source to trust, what "board-ready" means here, which sentence smells like a hallucination. You can't hand that over with a folder share. So don't try. Hand over a slice small enough that one teammate builds their own.

You built an agent that works. A teammate could use it. The obvious move is to clone the repo, grant the access, schedule the rollout — hand them the whole thing. It is the move a consultancy would sell you, and it is the move that fails. The agent works because of calibration that lives in you, not in the files: hundreds of sessions where you ran it, caught it wrong, and pushed back. Share the whole system and the teammate either trusts it blindly — wrong outputs go straight into the deck — or distrusts it entirely and reopens the spreadsheet. The calibrated middle takes about six months to grow. You can't ship six months.

The move a consultancy would sell — and why it fails

"Roll the agent out to the team. Train everyone. Big-bang launch, full access, change-management deck."

Access is the cheap half. The deck ships, the licences provision, the kickoff happens — and nothing reaches the people who'd actually use it, because absorption was never anybody's job. The bottleneck is organisational, not computational. So invert it: instead of asking what can I hand over, ask what can one person absorb this week. One teammate. One slice. One plan for who owns it. The unit of adoption is a person, not a team — the big-bang rollout has no unit at all, which is exactly why it dissolves.

01Name the teammate — and interview for the job.

Pick one person, not "the team." Then resist solving for your agent before you understand their work. Every job already has a current hire — for research, it's probably a trusted analyst, a Slack channel, and a morning coffee with the right person. The question isn't "will she use it?" but "what would make her fire the incumbent?" — and that answer is social nine times out of ten. Speed and quality are the obvious axes; you expected those. The vector that earns its keep is the third one the conversation surfaces: a dependency removed, an anxiety reduced, a scope expanded — never the one she names up front.

This week: write one sentence — "what job are they actually trying to get done?" — not what they'd use your agent for, but the outcome they're chasing. Text it to them and ask if it's right. One reply tells you whether you're designing for their job or your assumption of it.

02Choose the smallest surface that moves that job.

There is a closed set of four, and they are not interchangeable. Share context — curated sources plus the rules file — and they build their own agent; lowest lift, fastest calibration. Share a skill — one scoped capability, bounded residual risk. Share the output — push them the artefact; low trust cost, but it needs an owner on both ends forever. Share an interface — a pull surface they query on demand; highest engineering lift, keeps every internal tacit. Picking between them is the design decision, and the smallest surface that does the named job almost always beats the larger one — because a tight feedback loop on a context share gets them to their own calibration faster than a full system that overwhelms them on day one.

03Write the people plan — it weighs equal to the technical one.

Every agent-sharing project gets to a working technical plan within a week: artefact, sources, rules, cadence — those answer themselves fast. The people plan stalls, not because it's hard but because it requires naming names, and naming names surfaces politics. Owner, governance, operating, accountability, propagation — five columns, and propagation is where it dies: who owns this when you're on holiday, who trains the next person, what happens when the owner leaves. A perfect technical plan with no people plan is a PowerPoint. It ships; nothing changes. Give the two plans equal thickness, equal priority, equal space in whatever you present.

04Watch for calibration — the test is them using it without you in the room.

The demo is not the crossing. The first time she pulls it up with you nowhere near — that's the signal. Better: she corrects an output, reads it, finds it wrong, pushes back. Calibration is accumulating. Best: she names a limitation unprompted — "it's good for X, but I don't use it for Y." That's not scepticism, that's scoped trust, that's the wall crossed. The failure state is the inverse and it hides well: using it for everything (zero calibration) or nothing (the crossing failed) look identical from outside. So run the blunt negative test — if the only use you ever see is during a demo you arranged, the technical plan shipped and the absorption plan didn't. Arrange no more demos; watch what happens Monday without you there.

Then do it again for the next person. Each crossing is a vote on which surface shape your organisation can actually absorb — and that emerging pattern is your company's adoption strategy, whether you named it one or not. Without this move, every other pattern in your personal stack stays privately held.

Polanyi: we know more than we can tell. The whole-agent share fails because calibration is tacit — but a small enough surface lets the teammate build their own fast.

You cannot hand over calibration. You can only design the conditions for someone else to grow it — one person, one slice, one absorption plan at a time. That is what crossing the wall actually is: not the rollout, not the access grant, but the quiet morning a named teammate opens it alone, reads an output, and tells you where it's wrong.
