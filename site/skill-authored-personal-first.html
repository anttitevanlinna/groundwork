The principle: Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual.

A knifemaker who has never carried their own blade is selling you a guess. Forge a reusable skill by conversation — answered from your own repo, not a manual — then carry it on your own bench: install it to ~/.claude/skills/ and run it on the thing you just built, so you arrive at the team with evidence, not eloquence.

You'd already cut with it — before it ever left the shop. The nicks are the argument.
The bench · why a typed skill is hollow

You wouldn't buy a chef's knife from someone who's never cooked with one.

The honest knifemaker keeps a working blade on their own bench — the one they reach for to open boxes, strip wire, trim leather all day. That daily abuse is what tells them where the edge rolls, which grind chips, where the handle raises a blister by hour three. Only a blade that has lived on the maker's bench earns the right to leave the shop. So here is the question that decides everything about a skill you're about to author: why would you hand a teammate one you've never run yourself? You've already decided this recurring judgment is worth keeping — that's settled, not our subject. The only question that matters now is how you make the skill worth anything, instead of worth the paper a manual is printed on.

The trap · you become a documentarian

Open an editor and within two paragraphs you're a tech writer.

You start typing SKILL.md, and the prose drifts on its own toward a test strategy, the conventions a reasonable codebase might hold. What comes out is competent, generic, and useless — manual-wisdom, the stuff any blog post or any AI consultancy could hand you without ever having seen your repo. A typed skill is a document. It describes knives in general. Nobody's hand is in it. And the reason it's hollow is structural, not lazy: the value of this skill was never in the words. It lives in the conventions only your repo carries — the test framework you actually use, the mocking policy your reviews actually enforce, the three things a senior here flags that a senior elsewhere wouldn't blink at. That knowledge isn't written down anywhere to be copied. It has to be drawn out of the material — and a blank editor can't draw anything out of you. It just waits for you to dictate.

This is the documented failure mode of the move, not a stylistic quibble: the moment you reach for the editor to hand-write the markdown, you've left the forge for the filing cabinet. One is fed by your memory of how things are supposed to work; the other by the steel you actually brought.

The forge · talk it into existence, from your own steel

Don't type the skill. Talk it into existence.

Prompt the agent to author the skill by interview — one question at a time — and make it ask about this codebase, not skills in the abstract: "What runner do your tests actually use? When you mock, what's the rule for when you don't? Show me a test a reviewer here sent back — what was wrong with it? What does a green suite here mean that it wouldn't mean elsewhere?" You answer each one from the repo — you open the file, you quote the real convention, you paste the actual reviewer comment. The agent isn't downloading defaults; it's eliciting yours. Push back the instant it dumps all five questions at once: answer this one, ask the next.

A blade forged from steel you brought has properties the catalogue blade never will. The conventions are heated out of you by good questions and quenched into the skill — and what cools on the bench carries your answers, not a template's. Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual. This is the whole game.

A riff, not a measurement. Kieran Klaassen's compound-engineering idea is in the air here — the notion that every answer you give accretes, so the next skill starts from harder steel. Attributed as a framework riffed on, not a cited finding or a number. Treat it as the direction the bench leans, not a result anyone clocked.

Strike the edge · taste it before it leaves the pass

A line cook who plates a dish they haven't tasted is gambling with someone else's dinner.

Before the blade leaves your bench, put it on your own tongue. Make it disclose its own weakest section. Ask the agent flat out: "which part of this skill is thinnest — where would it mislead someone?" Then, when it confesses, push back on the confession. Argue the section is fine. Make it defend the critique or retract it. You're not looking for praise; you're looking for the spot the edge rolls under load. Strike harder when the read matters — tell it to over-flag ("find at least two sections that underdelivered, assume it's worse than it looks") or open a fresh session with no scrollback and have it read the skill cold. The blade that survives being struck against your own bench-vise is the one you'd hand over.

Authoring without invocation is theatre — a knife admired in its display case, never cut with.

Daily abuse · use it on the thing you just built

Here's the part nobody does — and the part that separates a forged tool from a forged signature.

Invoke the skill on the feature you literally just shipped. Drop it into personal scope so it's there every session without ceremony — ~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md, auto-discovered, no team rollout, no PR, no meeting. Just you and the work. Run the skill against the real change on your screen and watch what it does: where it nails a convention, where it whiffs, where it tells you something you'd forgotten you knew. That's the daily abuse on the maker's bench — the only test that counts. And the verdict is binary in a useful way: a skill that only ever produces "use the test pyramid" tells you the forge stayed cold — you answered from memory, not the repo. A skill that surfaces something codebase-specific — the integration boundary you actually cross, the review reflex this repo enforces — tells you the conversation reached the steel.

And notice what shipping-to-yourself-first actually is: not timidity, not "I'll do the safe small version." It's the cheapest proving ground in the building. When you eventually decide to bring this skill to the team — a different conversation, on a different day — you don't arrive with a draft and a hope. You arrive with a blade you've been cutting with for a week and the nicks to prove it. The diagram below draws that crossing as what it is: deliberately downstream, dashed, and far easier because you carry evidence.

The crossing · personal bench → shared kit

Name the team-worthiness. Don't force the crossing here.

Before any promotion, ask one question: is this codebase-specific — an auth pattern, an ADR convention, a named verifier — and therefore team-worthy? Or is it a personal working-style preference that should stay yours? Naming that difference precisely is the move; the team-worthy flag drifts to mush the instant you reach for a universal like "every team should know this." Name a codebase-specific call instead. The actual crossing is a later human conversation, not an automatic PR the moment the skill compiles — and it's far easier to have when you arrive carrying a blade that has already caught something real.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

The test isn't "looks sharp." It's "I've already cut with it."

You know the blade is real the same way the knifemaker knows: not because it looks sharp, but because you've already cut with it. Concretely: you ran the skill on the feature you just built — not on a hypothetical — and it raised something codebase-specific, not generic test-pyramid wisdom. You can say in one sentence whether it's team-worthy or personal, and why, naming a real call — an auth pattern, an ADR convention, a verifier — rather than a universal claim. The next session cold-reads it without being told, because it lives at user scope and is auto-discovered. And the negative test is the sharpest of the lot: if the SKILL.md was hand-typed in an editor and never invoked, the move didn't happen — you wrote documentation, not an instrument. This is the same discipline Agentic Engineering 101 trains the hour you author a test-strategy skill and run it on the very feature a staff engineer and a CISO are about to review.

Why
The principle

Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual.

A skill authored on your codebase is worth more than a perfect generic one, because the value was never in the words — it was in the conventions only your repo carries. The frontier's curated skills do the work you don't re-derive: invoke, don't re-derive. But the test discipline that catches this repo's mistakes can't be downloaded. It has to be elicited, one question at a time, from the person who knows how the code actually behaves. That's why authoring is a conversation, not a markdown file: typing produces a document; conversation produces an instrument.

And personal-first isn't timidity — it's the cheapest possible proving ground. You install to your own bench, cut it on the feature in front of you, strike its weakest part, and only then does the team conversation become an option, now backed by a thing that has already caught something real. A carried-and-cut blade is evidence; a draft is only intent — evidence beats eloquence.

One skill shipped beats three drafted for an audience.
No permission required

Every part of this move happens at personal scope. The blade hangs on your own ~/.claude/skills/ and the agent auto-discovers it by name — no admin, no rollout to wait on. The forging is a normal session conversation; the cut runs on a feature you already have. The only step that touches anyone else is the shared-kit promotion — and that's deliberately downstream, a later human conversation that's easier precisely because you arrive with a blade already proving itself.

Tonight

After your next coding session — while the work is still warm.

Ten minutes. The blade lives on your personal bench by dinner; no one had to approve a thing.

  1. Pick the one judgment the agent needed from you this session — the thing only you knew because only you know this repo.
  2. Ask the agent to author a skill for it by conversation — one question at a time. Answer every question from the actual files, not from memory. "ask me one at a time about how we test here, then write the SKILL.md to ~/.claude/skills/."
  3. Make it name its own weakest section, then push back on the critique until it either holds or breaks.
  4. Drop it in ~/.claude/skills/ and invoke it on the feature you just built. Watch where the edge rolls.

You'll know it's real the same way the knifemaker knows: not because it looks sharp, but because you've already cut with it.

Where this sits

Personal → team → company. At personal scope this turns a one-off judgment into a reusable, auto-discovered instrument. At company scope it's the entry point to kit accretion: every blade carried personal-first is a candidate the shared rack grows by, so a company's compounding capability rests on how reliably its personal benches feed the shared kit. This card is the explicit prerequisite for the crossing it makes possible — team-kit promotion.
