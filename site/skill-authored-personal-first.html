Ship the skill to yourself first

The principle: Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual.

Author a reusable skill by conversation — one question at a time, answered from your own repo — then install it to your own ~/.claude/skills/ and run it on the thing you just built, so you arrive at the team with evidence, not a draft.

It ran on the thing you just built — before anyone else ever saw it. The argument is in the loop.
The mechanism · conversation in, instrument out

Type it and you're a documentarian. Talk it and you're a builder.

There are two roads to a SKILL.md, and they look identical at the finish line and nothing alike in what they carry. Road one: you open an editor and hand-write the markdown. Now you're a documentarian, producing a tidy document that reads like every other testing guide on the frontier — generic by construction, because you wrote it from memory of how things are supposed to work. Road two: you ask the agent to interview you, and you answer from the repo in front of you. The output looks the same. It is not the same. The conversation captures judgment the editor never asked for.

The trap is that hand-typing feels like the rigorous choice — you, taking ownership, writing it down properly. It isn't. Authoring-by-typing is the documented failure mode here: the moment you reach for the editor, redirect to conversation. The agent's job is to drag the codebase-specific calls out of you one at a time — "what's your mocking policy?", "what does a review in this repo actually catch?" — and your job is to answer from how the repo behaves, not how the manual says it should. Push back when it dumps all five questions at once: answer this one, ask the next. The diagram below shows why the conversation road wins — it's the only one whose input is your repo.

Process diagram · 01Same file at the finish line. Typing pulls from your memory of the manual; conversation pulls from your repo — that's the only difference, and it's the whole difference.

A first draft from the agent is generic-risk by default — it has no way of knowing where it's thinnest until you make it look. So make it look. Ask the skill to disclose its own weakest section, then push back on the critique rather than accepting it; the mirror reflects what your own read would miss. Turn the heat up when the stakes are high: tell it to over-flag — "find at least two sections that underdelivered, assume it's worse than it looks" — or open a fresh session with no scrollback and have it read the skill cold. The grill is not optional politeness. It is the step that moves the skill off the first draft.

Authoring without invocation is theatre. You don't trust the skill until you've run it on something real.

And the only run that counts is on the work you just did. Invoke the skill on the feature you just built and ask, plainly: is this test strategy any good? A skill that only ever produces "use the test pyramid" tells you the authoring conversation stayed generic — you answered from memory, not from the repo. A skill that surfaces something codebase-specific — the integration boundary you actually cross, the review reflex this repo enforces — tells you the conversation reached the judgment. The diagram below draws the short arc, and the arrow from drafted to proven is not free: it costs you the grill and the invocation.

Process diagram · 02Drafted is generic-risk; the grill and the invocation are what earn the word "proven". Skip either and you shipped a draft wearing a skill's clothes.

Here is the part teams skip, and the part that compounds. Once the skill is proven, you don't have to do anything with it yet — personal-first is the principle, not a holding pen you're embarrassed about. It installs to your own ~/.claude/skills/, user-level, auto-discovered from any session by name. No one reviews it. No one approves it. And because you proved it on your own codebase before anyone saw it, the next move — naming whether it's team-worthy — is a calm one. The diagram below draws the loop the personal proving ground feeds.

The crossing · personal proving ground → team kit

Name the team-worthiness. Don't force the crossing here.

Before any promotion, ask one question: is this codebase-specific — an auth pattern, an ADR convention, a named verifier — and therefore team-worthy? Or is it a personal working-style preference that should stay yours? Naming that difference precisely is the move; the team-worthy flag drifts to mush the instant you reach for a universal like "every team should know this." Name a codebase-specific call instead. And the actual crossing is a later human conversation, not an automatic PR the moment the skill compiles — far easier to have when you arrive carrying a skill that has already caught something real.

Process diagram · 03The kit grows by accretion, not one big rollout. Every skill proven personal-first is a candidate the shared kit grows by — and each one raises the precedent the next authoring starts from.
What "Test" actually means · in practice

The test isn't "looks good." It's "it caught something I'd have missed."

You know the skill worked when you ran it on the feature you just built — not on a hypothetical — and it raised something codebase-specific, not generic test-pyramid wisdom. You can say in one sentence whether it's team-worthy or personal, and why, naming a real call — an auth pattern, an ADR convention, a verifier — rather than a universal claim. The next session cold-reads it without being told, because it lives at user scope and is auto-discovered. And the negative test is the sharpest of the lot: if the SKILL.md was hand-typed in an editor and never invoked, the move didn't happen — you wrote documentation, not an instrument. This is the same discipline Agents 101 trains the hour you author a test-strategy skill and run it on the very feature a staff engineer and a CISO are about to review.

Use when

You just did work where your judgment carried something the agent didn't have — a testing convention, a review reflex, a codebase-specific call — and you can feel it'll recur next sprint, on the next feature, for the next teammate.

Why
The principle

Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual.

"Your codebase teaches the skill, not the manual."

A skill authored on your codebase is worth more than a perfect generic one, because the value was never in the words — it was in the conventions only your repo carries. The frontier's curated skills do the work you don't re-derive: invoke, don't re-derive. But the test discipline that catches this repo's mistakes can't be downloaded. It has to be elicited, one question at a time, from the person who knows how the code actually behaves. That's why authoring is a conversation, not a markdown file: typing produces a document; conversation produces an instrument.

And personal-first isn't timidity — it's the cheapest possible proving ground. You install to your own scope, run it on the feature in front of you, grill its weakest part, and only then does the team conversation become an option, now backed by a thing that has already caught something real. A shipped-and-proven skill carries evidence; a draft carries only intent.

One skill shipped beats three drafted.
No permission required

Every part of this move happens at personal scope. The skill installs to your own ~/.claude/skills/ and the agent auto-discovers it by name — no admin, no team rollout, no platform change, no budget. The authoring is a normal session conversation; the invocation runs on a feature you already have. The only step that touches anyone else is the team-kit promotion — and that's deliberately downstream, a later human conversation that's easier precisely because you arrive with a skill already proving itself.

Where this sits

Personal → team → company. At personal scope this turns a one-off judgment into a reusable, auto-discovered instrument. At company scope it's the entry point to kit accretion: every skill proven personal-first is a candidate the shared kit grows by, so a company's compounding capability rests on how reliably its personal proving grounds feed the shared kit. This card is the explicit prerequisite for the crossing it makes possible — team-kit promotion.
