Pour the Sill,
Don't Read the Flood

When your agents out-produce reading, "review it all" quietly becomes the plan — and it is a losing one. The town that survives a flood it cannot watch decided, in concrete, what it would let pass. So do you: you stop being the watchman and become the engineer who poured the rim.

The situation

The river got bigger. You're still holding the same bucket.

Use this when your agents produce more than any human can read, and review it all has quietly become the plan. You feel it as a queue that won't drain — an agent, or a small swarm, drafts the memos, the clause analyses, the PRs, faster than the people downstream can absorb them. So you do the obvious thing harder: read everything, or sample at random, or trust-and-ship until something bites.

All three fail, and they fail structurally, not for lack of grit. Read-everything doesn't scale. Sample-randomly misses the wrong slice — errors cluster in the edge cases, not evenly across the pile. Trust-and-ship works right up until it doesn't. The failure mode this card owns has a name, and the name is volume.

CircleCI 2026 State of Software Delivery — observed workflow runs, not a survey (8M+ PRs across 5,000+ teams): workflow runs up 59% year over year while median throughput to main fell 7%. Generation surged; delivery sagged.

Rob Bowley's reading of that same dataset is the line that stings: only ~1 in 20 teams scaled both. The other nineteen are reviewing harder — standing at the riverbank with a bigger river and the same bucket. (practitioner reading, attributed to Bowley, not to CircleCI directly)

The move — a role change before a technique

The dam doesn't read the river

Go look at how a town survives a flood it cannot watch in person. Below a reservoir sits a morning-glory spillway — a great concrete bell-mouth, an inverted trumpet planted in the water. For most of the year it does nothing. The water sits below its rim and the structure is, to all appearances, asleep.

Then the snowmelt comes, the level climbs, and the instant it crosses the lip the bell-mouth lets through everything above that line and drops it down the throat, away from the town. Nobody is on duty deciding. No engineer reads each inch of rising water and votes. The decision was made years ago, in concrete — in the height of the sill and the flare of the mouth.

You are not the night-shift watchman reading the gauge. You are the engineer who poured the sill — and the analogy, not an assertion, is what changes your job.

Your work stops being review the output and becomes build the thing that decides what reaches you. The watchman scales with attention, and attention is the one resource multiplication eats alive. The sill scales with water it never sees. That is a reviewer turning into a system-designer — not by decree, but because the spillway has no role for a reader.

What to build — pour the sill

Three courses of concrete

Course 1 · set the height

Name the share you think is wrong — let that set the rim

Your reviewable slice is small; the rest is overflow you've been bailing by hand. Don't engineer to a tidy percentage you can't defend — name the order of magnitude (single digits) that would actually matter, and let it fix the height of the sill. The rim's height is a decision, not a measurement.

Course 2 · pour the bell-mouth

Put a first-pass agent in front of yourself

Have an agent review the agent's output before any human looks: find issues, verify to drop its own false positives, rank by severity. That agent is the bell-mouth, not you. But carry the blind spot out loud — the reviewer can hallucinate, and a bell-mouth that's miscast passes the wrong water with total confidence.

Stop here. Whether this judge earns the trust to filter on your behalf is not this card's question. Measure it in benchmark-the-judge — benchmark it there before you let it pour.

LLM-as-judge runs ~80–85% agreement with human judgment at a 500×–5000× cost cut — and humans agree with each other only ~81% of the time, so the judge approaches human-parity, it does not exceed a verified truth. (academic + practitioner sources; emerging, not settled)

Course 3 · bias the deafness

Set the sill non-linear, biased to under-react

A spillway is deliberately deaf to small rises — it has to be, or it would dump on every spring shower and the town would learn to ignore it. Tier by severity the same way: ignore the low-value alerts, let only the high-severity or low-confidence items cross the rim. Bias hard toward not-reacting to the small, hard toward catching the large. A filter that cries flood at a drizzle is one you'll route around within a week.

Where a real gauge exists — and where it doesn't

Some work carries an honest scalar: latency, conversion, training loss. There the filter is the number — run many candidates, look only at the ones that moved it. Most business judgment has no such gauge (marketing quality, legal accuracy, strategic fit). Don't paint a gauge on the wall to feel rigorous — fall back to the bell-mouth and the severity sill. A painted gauge floods the town on a clear day.

The test — does the sill hold?

You read dramatically less than your agents produce, and you can say which slice you read and why it was that slice — not "I skim it all."

When something breaks in production, you can point to where it sat below the rim — a recall gap you can name and raise the sill against, not a vague dread. (If you can't name where the bell-mouth would have missed, you don't have a filter, you have hope.)

A colleague can read your severity policy and predict what reaches a human without asking you — the rim's height lives in a file, not in your head.

Negative test: if you're still opening every PR "just to be safe," the concrete never set and the watchman never went home.

Why this is the move

95% accuracy at overwhelming volume is worse than 80% accuracy at a volume you can actually read.

Review is a flow constraint, not an attention problem — so the fix is a structure you pour, not effort you add. When generation roughly doubles and each unit carries more issues, the review burden multiplies, it doesn't add — and you cannot out-attention multiplication. No watchman reads a flood. The town that survives is the one that decided, in advance and in concrete, what it would let pass and what it would catch. That decision is your absorption capacity. Without it, faster generation isn't progress; it's just a higher river behind the same bucket.

The watchman's care is a fixed cost per inch of river. Multiply the river and meticulousness-per-unit can't keep up — the sill scales, the watchman doesn't. Your accuracy was never the asset. Your clearance rate was.
Monday

Take one agent output you produced this week. Do not review it. Instead, pour three lines of concrete — your first sill.

1

Set the rim

The one severity that would actually hurt if it shipped. That sets the height.

2

Pour the check

One check — a prompt to another agent, a grep, a threshold — that catches that one thing and nothing smaller.

3

Let the rest cross

Everything else: would I really have caught it by reading anyway? If not, let it through.

Those three lines are your first sill. You've stopped standing at the riverbank and started building the thing that decides what crosses.
