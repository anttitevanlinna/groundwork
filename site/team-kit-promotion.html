Promoting a skill to the team

We built good things. We failed to share them well. Cross-team knowledge transfer remains the unsolved problem. Agents can dissolve the technical barriers. The social and organisational barriers are still there.

The contrarian bet: the kit crosses bottom-up, never on a launch — it accretes one proven file at a time, and the announcement you were about to write is the part that fails.

A skill in your personal kit is already catching real things on real code. The move is not to roll it out — it is to let it cross the wall the same way it earned its keep: through one conversation, carried by the evidence already in your scrollback.

Everyone reaches for the launch. The launch is the part that fails.

There is a skill in your ~/.claude/skills/ directory — probably more than one — that has already caught a real thing, on real code, in your actual codebase. It encodes a convention your team argued over in two retros and then failed to apply the next sprint. You know it works because you have watched it work. Your teammate does not have it, and has no particular reason to know it exists. That gap is the wall — not political, not a process problem, just the ordinary distance between a working personal file and the shared surface where it could fire on every PR.

So you reach for the launch: a standup mention, a wiki page under "Useful tools," the ambitious version with a training session. Each of these asks people to adopt a system they did not build and have not tested on anything they own. The evidence is not theirs yet — and a busy engineer's prior for "this new process will save me time" is, deservedly, low. The wiki page goes stale. The same gap resurfaces next sprint. The announcement felt like the crossing; it was noise.

One of the big wants we had was to share learnings and good practices across sites and teams. We failed to imagine how that knowledge sharing could really work. Then we did not really action on that.

“Failing and Succeeding at the Same Time” · Dec 2025 — on cross-site knowledge sharing

The contrarian read: a kit does not get rolled out. It accretes — and the unit of accretion is one file that already proved itself, carried by one conversation.

Before you ship anything

Name what it is

Is this skill a codebase-specific convention — your auth pattern, your ADR rule, your named integration verifier — or a personal working-style preference, like your taste on test verbosity? Promote the first; keep the second to yourself. Over-eager promotion fills the team kit with one person's opinions; over-timid leaves real codebase knowledge locked in a file everyone else re-discovers the hard way.

The principle, not a staging area

Personal first, always

It lands in ~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md regardless. You run it on your own code, watch what it catches, and let the evidence accumulate in your sessions. This is the whole reason the crossing is cheap later: by the time you talk to a teammate, you are reporting a finding, not pitching an idea. The proof is already in the scrollback.

The step everyone treats as optional

Carry the evidence into one conversation

Not a roll-out — one teammate. You bring three things: what the skill caught, the convention it encodes, and where it lives. Then you open a normal code PR adding SKILL.md to .claude/skills/ in the repo, reviewed like any other diff. No sign-off, no policy doc. The evidence is the persuasion — most promotions stall here precisely because the author skipped this step, assuming the file would spread on its own.

Where a skill becomes a guarantee

Wire it into CI

A skill fires on someone's judgment; a rule fires on every PR. Once it has proven itself across a few sessions — ideally sharpened by a second author — a pre-commit hook or CI step invoking the check converts a personal win into a team guarantee. The convention argued over in two retros now fires silently before every merge, for every contributor, including the one who joined last month and never heard the argument. This is the one step that needs permission; arrive with a catch-rate number and the conversation is short.

Why bottom-up wins

A skill proven on one feature arrives with more authority than a policy doc written from first principles — the evidence is the persuasion.

The launch loses not because people are irrational but because they are busy, and a system they did not build has no claim on their attention. Accretion wins because each addition arrives with a wound it already healed. Done right, the kit's commit history reads like an archaeology of your actual production mistakes — and it carries more than one author's name. The loop runs in reverse too: every red CI build on agent-drafted code is diagnostic — if it caught a real mistake, ask what rule in CLAUDE.local.md would have caught it upstream, and if none exists, write it. Rules grow from real failures, never prophylactic policy.

Sometimes I think I should just give teams the minimum viable process and let them evolve it. Start with the simplest thing that could possibly work. Let them hit the constraints. Let them discover what they actually need, not what I think they need.

“The mechanical duck and processes” · Sep 2025 — on minimum viable process

There is a wall behind the wall.

The failure mode hides well because it looks like success: you mention the skill, a couple of people say "oh nice," it gets added to the wiki — and it never fires in anyone else's session. So make the test falsifiable. The pattern crossed when a teammate invokes the skill unprompted, in their own session, on a PR you did not write. If you have to mention it at standup for it to get used, only the announcement crossed. Better still: a teammate proposes a sharpening from their own invocation evidence — now they are in the encode loop, not just consuming it. Clearing that second wall is the whole point.

What to do Monday

Open ~/.claude/skills/ and find one skill you invoked at least twice this week. Decide: codebase-specific, or personal preference? If it is codebase-specific, draft a two-sentence opener — what it catches, and where it lives in the kit — and send it to one teammate before end of day. That is the entire move. Everything up to and through the conversation needs nothing but the evidence you already have; only the mandatory CI gate needs anyone's permission, and that ask is easy once a teammate has used the skill by choice.

The wall does not come down with a launch. It comes down one proven file at a time — and the next agent that cold-starts on the repo reads a kit that already knows what yours learned the hard way.
