Promote the personal win to the team

The principle: Accretion beats adoption — each addition arrives with evidence; wholesale roll-out arrives with a policy doc nobody asked for.

Move a skill or rule that is already doing work from your personal kit into the team's shared surface — one conversation, one PR, carried by evidence from real sessions.

The wall crossed when a teammate sharpened the skill from their own evidence — not when you announced it at standup.
The mechanism · why personal-first is the only safe order

The wall exists because adoption is hard. Accretion is not.

There is a skill in your ~/.claude/skills/ directory — probably more than one — that has already caught a real thing, on real code, in your actual codebase. It encodes a convention your team has argued over in two retros, then promptly failed to apply in the next sprint. You know it works because you've watched it work. Your teammate doesn't have it, and has no particular reason to know it exists. This is the wall. Not political. Not a process problem. Just the ordinary gap between a working personal tool and the shared surface where it could fire on every PR.

The instinct is to announce it in standup, or write a policy doc, or (the ambitious move) a full team roll-out with a wiki page and a training session. Each of these asks your teammates to adopt a system they didn't build and haven't tested on anything they own. The evidence isn't theirs yet. Adoption fatigue is what makes most good practices vanish between the standup mention and the next sprint. The mechanism that actually works is quieter and slower: one conversation, one PR, one person arrives at the skill the same way you did — through a real problem, not a policy doc.

A skill proven on one feature arrives with more authority than a policy doc written from principles — the evidence is the persuasion.

The scope decision · before you ship anything

Not every skill earns a team PR.

The first question at authoring time — before you write a line — is also the easiest one to skip: is this skill encoding a codebase-specific convention (your auth pattern, your ADR convention, your named integration verifier), or is it a personal working-style preference (your taste on function naming, your preference for test verbosity)? The distinction matters more than it looks. Over-aggressive promotion turns the team kit into a collection of one person's opinions. Over-timid leaves real codebase knowledge locked in a personal file forever, re-discovered the hard way by everyone else.

The rule is simple and it never changes: personal first, always. The skill goes to ~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md first — not as a staging area, but as the principle. You test it on your own code. You watch what it catches. You build the evidence. Then, when you walk into the team conversation, you're not pitching an idea — you're reporting a finding. The conversation is easy because the proof is already in the scrollback.

The CI surface · where skill becomes guarantee

A skill fires on judgment. A rule fires on every PR.

The highest-value target isn't the team conversation — it's the CI surface. Once a skill has proven itself across a few sessions and a second author has sharpened it, the question shifts from "do people know about this?" to "does this fire without anyone having to remember it exists?" A pre-commit hook invoking the check converts a personal win into a team guarantee. The convention that got argued over in two retros now fires silently before every merge, for every contributor, including the one who joined last month and never heard the argument.

The loop runs in the other direction too. Every CI red build on agent-drafted code is diagnostic: if it caught a real mistake, ask what rule in CLAUDE.local.md would have caught it upstream. If none exists, write it. Rules grow from real failures, not prophylactic policy. The team kit's commit history, if you're doing this right, should read like an archaeology of your actual production mistakes — each commit a lesson that didn't need to be learned twice.

The path from personal skill to team-enforced check is shorter than it looks — the skill is already proving itself; the only missing step is the conversation.

What "test" actually means · the falsifiable version

The promotion stalled at announcement.

There's a very specific failure mode for this pattern, and it hides well because it looks like success. You mention the skill in standup. A couple of people say "oh nice." Someone adds it to the team wiki under "Useful tools." The skill never fires in anyone else's session. The announcement felt like the crossing; it was just noise.

The test that tells you the pattern actually crossed is harder to fake: a teammate invokes the skill unprompted, in their own session, on a PR you didn't write. If you have to mention it at standup for it to get used, the habit didn't cross — only the announcement did. Better still: a teammate proposes a sharpening from their own invocation evidence. At that point they're in the encode loop, not just consumption. That's the wall behind the wall, and clearing it is the whole point.

Use when

A rule or skill that works for you is already doing work your teammates re-discover the hard way. Especially after: a sprint where someone encoded a real rule; a CI red build the loop should have caught; a kit-consolidation point where CLAUDE.md still reflects defaults, not accumulated wisdom.

Build

A PR adding SKILL.md to .claude/skills/ in the repo · a commit adding the convention to AGENTS.md · a pre-commit hook or CI step invoking the check on every PR · the kit's commit history showing accretion from >1 author.

Test

A teammate invokes the skill unprompted — it fired in their session.
CI catches what it was designed to catch on a PR you didn't write.
Only invoked when you mention it at standup.

Core moves

The crossing, in sequence

What changes = who benefits + what fires automatically.
1

Name what it is before you ship

Codebase-specific convention (team-worthy) or personal working-style preference (not)? The distinction prevents both failure modes.

2

Personal first, always

Lands in ~/.claude/skills/ regardless. Not a staging area — the principle.

3

Carry evidence to the conversation

What it caught. The convention it encodes. Where it lives. Evidence is the persuasion.

4

PR to the team kit

Normal code PR to .claude/skills/ or AGENTS.md. No platform, no budget, no sign-off.

5

Promote to CI surface

Pre-commit hook or CI step. Skill→hook converts a win into a guarantee. Bring a catch-rate number.

Monday move: Open ~/.claude/skills/. Find one skill invoked ≥twice this week. Codebase-specific or personal preference? If codebase-specific, draft a two-sentence team-conversation opener: what it catches + where it lives in the kit. Send it to one teammate before end of day. That's the whole move. No platform, no budget, no sign-off — everything up to and including the conversation needs nothing but the evidence. A mandatory CI gate is the only step that needs permission — and that conversation is easier when you arrive with a cycle-time number and a demonstrated catch rate.

Why
The principle

Compound engineering — each unit of work makes the next cheaper; the mechanism is the artefact the next agent reads.

"The path from personal skill to team-enforced check is shorter than it looks — the skill is already proving itself; the only missing step is the conversation."

Wholesale promotion fails because it asks people to adopt a system they didn't build. Nobody rejects it outright — they just don't use it, and the wiki page goes stale, and the next sprint the same gap resurfaces. Accretion works because each addition arrives with evidence. A skill proven on one feature, authored through conversation with the codebase, arrives with more authority than a policy doc written from first principles. The evidence is the persuasion — not because people are irrational, but because they're busy and their prior for "this new process will save me time" is deservedly low.

At team scale, the compounding surface is the shared kit and the rate is set by how reliably personal wins cross the wall. Most stall because the author never treats the team conversation as a required step — it feels optional, a nice-to-have. It isn't. The conversation is the mechanism. Without it, every engineer is perpetually re-discovering the same gaps, and your kit's commit history shows one author's name.

The promotion stall is usually not political — it's structural. Build the crossing move in.
