Failure-mode diagnosis

The principle: the trace rules, the summary doesn't — the closing line is the run pleading its own case; the commits, diff and scrollback have nothing to gain by how they read.

When a long-running run comes back, its closing summary is a director's cut — the bad take quietly dropped, the snag framed as a near-win — so don't judge the run by it. Open the actual trace — commits, diff, scrollback — and read it three times, one question per pass, until a vague "something's off" becomes a located fault you can point at by turn number. Own this read at your own desk first; promote the diagnosis into a shared verifier and the same discipline climbs from your desk to the team's pipeline to the company's quality bar — quality claims stop resting on a self-report nobody audited.

The summary is one line. The trace runs the length of the strip — three reads, each pins one moment in the run.

Here is the whole closing report, in full: "✓ shipped most of it, hit a minor snag on X." One reassuring line — and the most edited thing in the pile, written by the same process that did the run, under the same pressure to look helpful. So when something half-broke it doesn't say "I broke this"; it frames a partial failure as a partial success. That tilt is a practitioner observation about how agent self-reports read, not a measured finding — but once you've watched the trace behind a few of them, you stop judging the run by its closing line. Three passes, three rows, one question each. The naming of the lenses convergent practitioner pattern isn't an official Claude Code taxonomy — it's three recurring shapes a practitioner re-discovers by reading their own trace, named so each pass asks one clean question instead of one anxious blur. The read across all three is fast precisely because each lens has a job: it stops at the first moment that answers its question and pins it. A vibe can't be acted on; a pinned turn can.

The move most diagnoses skip · rank, don't inventory

turn 27 — the goal drift at commit 4d2c.

Rot and the null-check are real, but they ride downstream of the swap: once the run took the simpler path, every later turn was working the wrong task. So rank them — which one cost the most? The diagnosis isn't an inventory; it's a pointer to the single thing the next run's verifier has to catch. List all three and you build a verifier that catches everything and tightens nothing. Over-diagnosis is its own failure mode, and the comfortable one — listing everything feels safer than committing to a call. One line of why it cost most, and you're done.

Monday move · the next run you read

Open the last long-running run you sent off — the PR, the branch, the RUN-NOTES. Don't read its summary — read the trace it summarised. Open the actual diff and scrollback and read them three times, one question each: where did it drift from the goal, where did it lose the thread, where does the output look right but isn't. Under each, write one verbatim quoted moment — paste the commit line, the scrollback turn, the diff line, with its turn number. Then mark the single failure that cost the most. And read it as instrumentation, not blame: if you ran the task un-packaged — no reference, no plan file, no external verifier — it was supposed to underdeliver, and you ran it that way to watch how this agent fails on this task before you spend effort on guardrails. You walk away with a half-page three-lens diagnosis — a fault you can point at, not a vibe — and you know exactly which verifier to shape next.

Why

The summary is what the agent wants you to feel. The trace is what happened.

The reason is structural, not moral. A long-running run's closing summary is generated by the same process that produced the run, under the same pressure to look helpful — so it tilts toward framing a snag as a near-win. That tilt is a practitioner observation about why self-reports read the way they do, not a measured finding. The summary isn't a neutral account; it's the run pleading its own case. The only artefact with no stake in how it reads is the trace itself: the commits that landed, the diff that shipped, the turn where the goal quietly changed.

And the three lenses are the cure read backwards convergent practitioner pattern: a reference re-pins the goal each time it slips (catches drift), a plan file holds state across the long run (catches rot), an external verifier tests the output against intent (catches plausible-but-wrong). Diagnosis and cure are one taxonomy read from two ends. No benchmark told you which moment to pin — the trace did.

"Is this good?" is the wrong question. Ask: what went wrong, and where?
