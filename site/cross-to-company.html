Cross to the company layer

The principle: build on standards, not platforms — the company layer isn't a product you buy, it's conditions you create.

Make personal wins become how the whole company works — by setting shared standards, making governance the buying test, and naming a person who builds the human network and then steps back. Be honest about the top rung: there is no off-the-shelf company layer yet — promotion means rebuild, not upgrade. The product is a frontier; the conditions are buildable Monday.

Personal → team is lit and solid. Team → company is honestly unfinished — but the standards root crosses every band.
Use when · the wall you hit within weeks

The personal wins work. Now the question is "how does the COMPANY work?"

Here is the moment, and it arrives faster than anyone budgets for. Builders go through a couple of modules, build personal agents that genuinely work, and then — almost in the same breath — ask the three questions no platform answers together: where does this live, how do I share it, can it run when I'm not running it? At a Nordic security-software org that ran this exact sequence, people hit that gap organically, within weeks. It isn't a motivation problem. The wall is structural: across the major platforms, the personal, team, and company tiers are different products on different runtimes. There is no smooth escalator from your laptop to the enterprise.

The first thing those builders asked for wasn't a platform to buy — it was a pipe to connect: "we need MCPs for Snowflake and Salesforce." An agent that runs on sample data is a toy; the wall announces itself as a request for plumbing, not a request for procurement.

It shows up at three predictable moments. When a team's accreted kit is plainly worth more than the org's defaults but has no home above the repo. When shadow agents quietly proliferate — one vendor reported around 500,000 agents running internally, 29% of them with no IT approval. And when leadership asks for a "rollout plan" and reaches for a change manager or a platform license. That last reach is the move that fails. The instinct to buy the company layer or mandate the company layer is exactly the instinct this card is here to interrupt.

Frontier · not a tier

No off-the-shelf company layer yet — promotion means rebuild, not upgrade. An off-the-shelf, promote-don't-rebuild company layer largely does not exist across the major platforms today. One possible exception — Anthropic's Antspace with bring-your-own-cloud, same artefact and deploy protocol — is still staging, not production. The move now is to create the conditions, not buy the layer.

Process diagram · 01Vendor tiers break at every wall; standards cross intact. Promotion on a vendor tier means re-author everything — promotion on a standard means re-host the same artefact.

So what changes between the moves? The scope of governance widens, and the question of who sets the standard moves from you to the org. Start by naming the frontier out loud — say the quiet part before anyone signs anything: you cannot promote a working personal agent into a governed company one, you start over. Knowing that stops you over-investing in a tier you'll only rebuild. Then make the one bet that survives the rebuild: build on standards, not a vendor's company tier. MCP for tools, Agent Skills and AGENTS.md for behaviour. There are 6,400-plus MCP servers now spanning all four major platforms, and — as of 2026 — the standards war looks to be consolidating under the Linux Foundation's AI agent foundation (the early signal is consolidation, not a settled outcome). The tool integrations transfer even when the runtime doesn't — that is the whole difference between "promotion = re-author" and "promotion = re-host."

The third move is a re-aim of the buying criterion. The axis of competition has moved from which model is smartest to how you run agents safely, affordably, and governably across the enterprise. When you do evaluate a company layer, score it on identity for non-human agents, cost governance, data residency, audit trails, and permission granularity — governance first, model cleverness last. The four-layer stack is Models → Protocols → Governance → Applications, and the gap between protocols and governance is precisely where most enterprise agent efforts stall. The fourth and fifth moves are human, not technical, and we'll come to why that matters.

None of these is a purchase. The company layer accretes from standards plus a human network — and the loop tightens each pass, as governed agents replace shadow ones and peer adoption carries the next team.

The first company-layer act is inventory, not procurement. Non-human identities can outnumber humans ~50:1 · 82% of executives believe they're protected · 24% have actual visibility (one vendor's reporting). You will undercount. That undercount is the point of starting.

Now the move most leaders get backwards. When the org asks for adoption, the reflex is to appoint a change manager and run a top-down communication plan. Appoint a conditions-creator instead, not a change manager. Someone sets the tools, the boundaries, and the peer infrastructure — and then steps back. This is counterintuitive enough that it's worth saying plainly: in one study of agent adoption, leadership communication had zero direct effect on adoption once you controlled for peer influence. Peers drove it. The company layer is a human layer first — pick approved tools and data policies, find the natural network hubs (not the volunteers, not the managers), give them time and recognition, and measure peer spread rather than course completion.

One study · our label

"Conditions-creator" is our name for the role, not an industry-standard title — the pattern recurs across cases but isn't a converged finding. The peer-influence numbers (+8.9 percentage points overall, +10.4 for agents specifically) come from one study (Microsoft / HBR, N≈557), not from "research shows." Treat governance-first buying as the criterion you should use, not as documented buyer behaviour — that's a vendor-and-analyst bet, with no verified evidence buyers actually evaluate governance first.

Use when

The personal wins are real and the team is using them — and the question has become "how does this become how the company works," not just how this corner works. Shadow agents are multiplying with no home above the repo.

Build

A one-page standards bet, a governance scorecard, a conditions-creator mandate — and an agent inventory that goes from "we don't know what's running" to a governed, visible fleet.

Test

A team you didn't tell adopts your standard without being mandated.
Not a license bought + a memo sent.

Test · how you know it crossed

You know it crossed when a team in a different part of the org adopts an agent built to your standard without being told to — peer influence carried it, not an executive memo. When a tool integration survives a runtime change: you moved hosts or tiers and the MCP and Agent Skills work transferred instead of being rebuilt. When the sprawl number goes down — shadow agents become governed agents you can see, name, and attribute. And when a company-layer purchase is deferred or scoped small because the team correctly read it as a non-two-way-door decision in an immature market.

The negative test is the honest one. If "the company layer" is a license someone bought and a mandate someone issued, and adoption is still happening only where you point at it in town halls — the cross stalled at procurement. The artefact crossed; the company didn't. Progress is made by betting small, not by signing a platform.

The compounding loop · the layer assembles itself

The company layer isn't bought. It accretes.

Here is the reframe that makes the whole move land. The company layer was never primarily a thing you procure; it's a thing that accretes from standards plus a human network, and the loop tightens with each pass. A personal win lands. The team kit accretes around it. Because it was built on standards, it carries forward when the runtime changes. It gets governed and inventoried, so it becomes visible rather than shadow. Then peer adoption carries it into a new part of the org — and that new corner starts not from zero but from the shared standard. The loop closes, and each turn the company looks a little more like the thing you were tempted to buy.

Why
The principle

Build on standards, not platforms. The company layer is the headline promise — and the frontier nobody sells you honestly.

"There's no escalator from personal to company — the tiers are different products. So stop shopping for the company layer and start building the conditions for it."

Every vendor wants you to start on their platform, so every roadmap implies a smooth personal→team→company escalator. There is no escalator: the tiers are different products, and promotion means rebuilding. The rational response to an immature market is multi-vendor hedging — one large firm ran nine-figure annual spend with one model provider and partnered with another at the same time. Betting on standards, not platforms, is how you keep your options open while the market is pre-chasm.

The deeper mechanism is that the company layer was never primarily technical. In the one study we have, peer influence — not leadership communication — drove adoption, and trust in an agent is earned through observed competence over time — not granted by a governance dashboard. So the move a builder-leader can make now isn't to buy the layer. It's to create the conditions: set the standard, make governance the buying test, and appoint the person whose actual job is to step back. The product company layer is a frontier; the conditions for it are buildable Monday.

Stop shopping for the layer. Build the conditions for it.
Monday move · no purchase required

Open a doc and write one sentence: "We build agents on MCP and Agent Skills / AGENTS.md, not on any single vendor's company tier." Then list the agents you already know are running in the org — you'll undercount, which is the point. Send both to whoever could be your conditions-creator with one question: "Who are the three people other teams actually listen to?" That names your standard, surfaces your sprawl, and starts your peer network.

No-permission framing. Writing the standard is a doc · auditing sprawl is read-only · naming a conditions-creator is an org choice, not a procurement. Only the governed company-tier product needs budget and sign-off — and that's a non-two-way-door decision in a pre-chasm market, so the honest play is to defer it, bet small, and arrive with an inventory and a standards bet in hand rather than a vendor's roadmap.
