Scope an agent your org can trust

The principle: You can't tell if your agent is safe by looking at its output. The failure modes don't live where you can read them.

Make an agent safe to point at real systems by naming its residual risk — running the check yourself, not waiting for a clean bill of health that, for a non-deterministic system, will never arrive.

The agent isn't trusted because it looks fine. It's trusted because its boundary, and its one named residual, are on record.
The mechanism · why the output can't tell you

The failure isn't in the output. It's in what you can't see.

Here's the uncomfortable part of pointing an agent at anything real: the demo is no evidence. The agent reads an inbox, a CRM, a repo, and it answers beautifully. The pull is to wire it to act — route the thread, update the ticket, send the draft. But the failure modes that matter are not in the answer you can read. They live in what an attacker buried inside a retrieved email, in what the agent quietly decided to do with a tool, in what a borrowed skill carried in. You can re-read the output a hundred times and never meet them. That is why the CTO instinct — "wait for the security review to come back clean" — never fires: for a non-deterministic system, the clean report does not exist to wait for.

So the check carries two lenses, not one. A policy lens asks what your org has already decided is off-limits — the data-usage, security and AI-use rules you point the agent at as source material. An agent-risk lens asks the question classical perimeter security never thought to: is this input trying to steer or leak the agent? The two see different things, and the gap between them is the whole point. Name the agent-risk patterns against your agent — and there's a closed set the source teaches as the working vocabulary: prompt injection direct (planted in the user's own input), prompt injection indirect (planted in a source the agent retrieves and reads), secrets-in-context, tool-confusion, and skill supply-chain. Both injection classes turn well-formed English into the attack surface — which is exactly why the firewall shrugs. The attack is not malformed; it reads like a perfectly polite sentence. Walk each pattern against the specific agent and you stop reasoning about agents in general.

Closed set · vocabulary

These five risk patterns — and the five mitigations below — are the named sets from the source's R4 check: a working vocabulary, not an exhaustive taxonomy of every possible agent attack or defence. Name them faithfully and completely; don't treat either list as the whole universe.

Now the move most teams skip: give every rule three rows, not two. Compliant, violating, and "I can't tell." A binary classifier forces the ambiguous cases into false confidence — it has to pick a side, so it picks wrong with a straight face. The third row catches them and hands them back with the ambiguity intact, named, and addressable. Most rows, run honestly, land on the third. That's not the check failing. That is the first honest map of the surface — and "I can't tell" is a valid, useful output for an agent to produce.

A binary verdict is a confident lie about an ambiguous input. The third row is where the work actually lives.

The upgrade · five mitigations narrow the surface

Risk gets smaller and named — never zero.

Once a risk is named, you clip it. Pick from the closed set of five mitigations: scope (narrow what it can reach), split (separate the agent that reads from the one that acts), filter (strip the poison before it enters context), gate (plan-mode review — the agent proposes, you approve, only then it executes), and review (a second pass before the action lands). Each clips one edge of the blast radius. Crucially, these sit on top of your existing controls — network, IAM, logging, supplier review — they never replace them. Loop design is additive. A leader who concludes "the agent check replaces security" ships a breach.

The compounding loop · what survives the session

The residual you name today is the starting line tomorrow.

After the mitigation, something is still true. Write it down — on record, owned, dated. The report must not pretend a narrower instruction erased the risk; it shows what changed and what stayed. Then comes the move that turns a one-time audit into a standing boundary: compound the operating rule back into the project's CLAUDE.md — what this agent's failure modes are, what to check before it touches the system. The next session reads it on cold start and begins at a stricter line. The loop closes through the rules file in the soil; each pass, the starting line is stricter and the residual list is shorter and more honest.

Use when

The agent is about to touch real systems, real data, or a decision someone else relies on — and "the output looks fine" is the only check you have. Recurs at every new connector, action class, data zone, or team inheriting the deployment.

Build

A reusable security check as a personal skill (both lenses, travelling); a report with residuals (three verdicts per rule + the risk you accepted, the one you mitigated, what changed); and operating rules in CLAUDE.md future sessions load first.

Test

The report contains at least one residual you cannot flatten.
Not a clean sheet of all compliant.

Core moves

The action, in sequence

Two lenses, one check you authored, three verdicts. You didn't become a security specialist — you knew what mattered and dictated it.
1

Run the policy files raw

Point it at your policies, once. See the baseline the reusable check must beat.

2

Name the five risk patterns

Direct · indirect injection · secrets · tool-confusion · supply-chain. Walk each against your agent.

3

Give every rule three rows

Compliant · violating · "I can't tell." Most rows land on the third.

4

Mitigate one

Scope · split · filter · gate · review. On top of existing controls, never instead.

5

Name what remains

The residual, owned and dated. Then compound the rule into CLAUDE.md.

run the check→surface the residual→mitigate one→name what remains→compound

Then it's a standing boundary, not a one-time audit: compound interest on a residual list. The next agent you build starts at a stricter line because of this one.

Monday move: pick one agent you already use that touches real data. Open a file · three columns: rule · verdict · evidence · run the five risk patterns · write compliant / violating / "I can't tell." You'll land on "I can't tell" more than you expect — that's the first honest map. Pick the residual that bothers you most, write one sentence naming it. No platform, no budget, no sign-off.

Test · how you know it's scoped

The honest tell: the agent-risk lens caught something the policy lens shrugged at — or vice versa. Blend them and you'd miss it. In one team's worked example from the cookbook, a procurement-blocker thread read like routine billing all the way through; the only signal was a watched account name on a buried CC, and that match was what the policy lens caught while the risk lens was still shrugging. Blended lenses route it low-priority and miss it.

And the negative test: if every row came back compliant or violating and none came back "I can't tell," you forced false confidence. The third row exists precisely because a non-deterministic system produces ambiguity — hiding it is the failure.

Illustrative · one team

The procurement-blocker example — and the rough shape of "about five 'I can't tell' threads a week, a few of them the most important" — is one pseudonymised team's experience as told in the cookbook (a B2B SaaS support team), illustrative of the shape, not a benchmark or a rate to expect.

Why
The principle

Certainty about agent safety is a fantasy. The discipline is running the check — not waiting for it to come back clean.

"A scoped agent isn't one with no risk. It's one whose residual risk is named, owned, and visible to the next session."

None of this is exotic to a regulated firm. Normal security work already carries residual-risk vocabulary, accepted-risk registers, named owners — every SVP in a Nordic bank or insurer has met the concept. Agent security inherits all of it and adds three things on top: non-determinism, unbounded text inputs, and emergent tool use. You never get a proof. You get a loop.

Most teams stall right here — they treat the absent clean report as a blocker instead of treating the named residual as the deliverable. One footnote on the vocabulary: STRIDE shows up only as historical background — adapted from Microsoft's SDL, not used as the audit's scaffold here. R4 names the attack classes directly on purpose, so the lens can't collapse into closed-loop self-grading.

"I can't tell" is a valid agent output — and usually the most valuable one.
Where it sits · personal → company

This is the foundation guardrail layer. Run on your own agents, the check needs no permission — but its output travels: the named-residual report plus the CLAUDE.md rule are exactly what let a CTO put agents near regulated company systems with a documented, inheritable risk position instead of an unprovable promise of safety. Scope-for-trust is what every higher-autonomy and company-wide deployment rests on — the prerequisite for earning the next autonomy rung, and the boundary the whole organisation stops re-arguing once it's on record.
