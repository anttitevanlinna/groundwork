Earn the next rung

The principle: Trust is earned, not granted — no benchmark proves an agent is reliable for YOUR process; only observed performance on your data does.

Turn every permission decision — let it act, or only propose? — into a ladder you climb one rung at a time, by building the check that would have caught the failure before you climb.

Rung 4 is boring — the failure mode fired in February, the check caught it, no drama. That's the condition for climbing.
The mechanism · the jump that breaks things

The gap isn't access. It's trust.

Almost every organisation with a working pilot has the same failure mode. The demo goes well, excitement builds, someone decides to skip the boring middle part and push the agent from "it drafts, we review" to "it acts, we check the logs." The problem is not the agent — it's that nobody has actually seen the failure mode in their data, their edge cases, their org's particular flavour of weird. The move from rung 1 to rung 5 in one leap is not bold. It's just skipping the evidence collection.

Five independent research streams — legal, cross-industry, security, finance, software — all find the same pattern: access to AI is high and rising (54–95% of organisations have something deployed); autonomous trust is stuck at 5–22%. The gaps are enormous: 61 points in legal, 80 in cross-industry, 66 in security. This is not a slow adoption curve. It's an org-learning bottleneck. The bottleneck is not access. It is the absence of a protocol for building observed competence between pilot and deploy. The autonomy ladder is that protocol, made explicit and auditable.

No governance platform can grant trust. No vendor benchmark proves an agent is reliable for YOUR process. Trust is earned the same way it is between humans: observed performance, over time, in your context.

The rung-climb protocol · what "earned" means exactly

Run the proposal version first.

Before any rung promotion, run the agent in proposal mode at the new rung — it produces exactly what it would do, you observe it, you don't apply it. This is not risk-aversion theatre. It is the only way to see the failure mode in your data, not the vendor's benchmark. Vendor benchmarks are measured on the vendor's test set. Your calendar, your Slack history, your ticket queue, your customers' edge cases — these are not in the test set. The failure mode in your context only becomes visible when you run your context.

The rung is earned when three conditions hold simultaneously: (1) the proposal version passed N times with no surprise — N higher for higher blast radius; (2) the failure mode now has an explicit functioning check — the bad version would have been caught; (3) that check has fired at least once on real input. Not conceptually. Actually fired. Elapsed time is not a condition. Vendor confidence scores are not a condition. The check firing on real input is the condition.

The check that catches the failure is not overhead. It is the durable institutional artefact. The next engineer who inherits the deployment doesn't just get an agent — they get a trust map that shows which rungs have been earned, what check is in place at each one, and when it last fired. That's the difference between tribal knowledge and a transferable operating model.

The artefact · what the check actually is

The failure mode is the specification.

Designing the check that would have caught the failure is not a defensive move. It is the moment you understand the agent well enough to give it more autonomy. The check encodes your accumulated knowledge of where the agent fails on your specific process. An agent without checks isn't "low overhead." It's just an agent whose failure modes haven't been documented yet.

The check needs to be concrete and executable, not a policy statement. "Review high-stakes emails" is not a check — it's a thought you had once. A check is something that runs: a judge that flags sentiment drift, a classifier that catches blast radius, a simple rule that blocks actions touching more than N records. The criterion is equally simple: would this check have caught the bad version, reliably? If yes, you have a check. If the answer is "probably," you have a hypothesis that needs to fire on real input before it earns that status.

What "boring" means in practice

The goal is a boring check.

"Boring" is the operational test for rung advancement. Not "the agent did well this week." Not "we're comfortable." Boring means: the check fires, catches the bad version, and nobody is surprised. The failure mode has become a known quantity. It appears, the check catches it, you log it, you move on. The anxiety around "what if it does something wrong?" has been replaced by a specific, answerable question: "did the check fire today?"

This matters especially at the transition from rung 3 to rung 4 — from proposal to action. That's the rung where the agent stops asking and starts doing. The temptation is to reason about it in the abstract: "the blast radius is low, the actions are reversible, we're comfortable." The discipline is to run the proposal version N times until the boring condition holds, then flip the switch. The difference between these two paths, at scale, across a dozen agents, is the difference between an org that owns its AI deployment and one that hopes for the best.

The ceiling in the planning template — call it 95%, though it's an illustrative template number and yours will differ — is worth noting only for what it isn't: it's never 100%. Not because the remaining few percent will inevitably fail, but because architecture that assumes 100% trust is architecture that has stopped watching. Rung 6 — escalate the rest — is always on. That's not a limitation. That's the design.

Use when

You have a working agent and a permission decision: let it act, or only propose? This question recurs at every new scope — new connector, new action type, new team, new blast radius. The temptation is to jump because the demo went well. The risk is you haven't seen the failure mode in your data yet.

Build

A per-deployment trust/permission map — one table per agent: Rung · Action class · Current status · Check in place · N clean runs · Blocker to next rung. Makes the current rung visible to the next engineer, compliance, quarterly review.

Test

Three conditions simultaneously: proposal passed N times; failure mode has a functioning check; check fired on real input. Not elapsed time. Not vendor benchmarks. Observed competence on your process.

Core moves

The climb, step by step

The protocol, not the permission.
1

Name the current rung

Honestly. Most agents are rung 1 or 2. The map starts here.

2

Run proposal mode at next rung

See what it would do. Don't apply it yet. See the failure mode in your data.

3

Design the check

The bad version would have been caught by: ___. Write that sentence. Make it executable.

4

Let the check fire

On real input. Not conceptually. At least once. Now you have evidence.

5

Reach boring

N clean runs, no surprises. The check fires, catches it, nobody panics.

6

Advance the rung

Update the trust map. The check stays. It is now institutional memory.

proposal runs→failure surfaces→check designed→boring→rung advanced

Then repeat at the next rung. Each climb leaves a check: compound institutional knowledge of where this agent fails in your context.

Monday move: Pick one agent you already use. Write its current rung (honestly — most are 1 or 2). Write the one failure mode you've observed or can clearly imagine. Write one sentence: "the check that would have caught it is ___." That's your rung-2 spec. No infra, no budget, no vendor contract. One sentence.

Why
The principle

The bottleneck is not access. It is the absence of observed competence between pilot and deploy.

"The move is not to jump from rung 1 to rung 6. The move is to earn the next rung."

Five independent research streams — legal, cross-industry, security, finance, task-specific software — all find the same structural gap between access and autonomous trust. The numbers vary by domain but the shape is constant: organisations that have AI deployed (54–95%) vastly outnumber those that trust it to act autonomously (5–22%). The gap is not ignorance. It's the absence of a protocol for building evidence between "it demos well" and "it acts in production." The autonomy ladder makes that evidence-building explicit, step by step, artefact by artefact.

The Moltke parallel is structural, not borrowed prestige: Auftragstaktik — mission command — worked on the same principle. Autonomy isn't granted top-down at appointment. It is earned bottom-up through demonstrated performance over time, at increasing responsibility. Skipping the observation phase produces fragile systems whether the context is Prussian cavalry or production AI. The analogy clarifies the mechanism; it doesn't replace the evidence.

Monday move · no-permission framing

Pick one agent you already use. Doesn't matter which. Open a blank document. Write: current rung: ___. Write: one failure mode I've seen or can clearly imagine: ___. Write: the check that would have caught it: ___. That's the rung-2 design spec.

No org sign-off required. Rungs 1 and 2 are purely additive — checks on work you already do. No platform, no budget, no vendor contract. The trust map is a personal audit doc until you choose to share it. The first move costs one sentence. Don't upgrade to rung 3 until that check exists and has fired.
