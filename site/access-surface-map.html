Map the access surface first

The principle: Map what you're protecting before you name what could go wrong.

Before you threat-model, send a fresh subagent to walk the whole feature and map who can reach what — so your own attention stays free for the two surfaces only you can name: the one it under-weighted, and the one it missed. Breadth is borrowed; judgment is yours.

The breadth is borrowed. The two amber threads are yours — the surface it drew too thin, and the one it never drew.
The mechanism · why you map before you threat-model

STRIDE has nothing to chew on until the surface is mapped.

Here's the trap, and almost everyone walks into it: you've a feature shipping this week — a webhook handler, a new route, a tenant-scoped query — a staff engineer and a CISO will review it, and you're quietly nervous one of them will catch a surface you didn't think to look at. So you reach for STRIDE, the six-category threat checklist. That's the trap. STRIDE without a surface map is pub-quiz threat-modelling — six categories walked against a vibe. The thing you're missing isn't the threat list; it's the map of what the threats land on. You can't reason about spoofing a boundary you never wrote down.

So map first. But mapping every entry point, boundary, data tier and auth checkpoint a feature touches is a long, structured breadth read — and breadth reads are exactly what you hand to a fresh subagent. Invoke a curated access-control skill, name the feature by route or a couple of symbols, and let it find the code itself. It burns its whole context window walking the tree; yours stays clean for the judgment only you carry. This is the breadth-first-to-a-subagent split: the wall of structured output leaves the main thread on purpose, and you stay rested for the part a generic reader structurally can't do. Process diagram · 01Same feature, two readers. Breadth from the skill, depth from you — they converge on the delta note, and that join is what STRIDE reads.

Make the read cover six surfaces, not five: entry points · trust boundaries crossed (service, network, tenant, role) · data read and written with its sensitivity tier · authorization checkpoints, separated into present, assumed, and delegated · external tool and connector calls · and the load-bearing one — anything that bypasses, defers, or inherits an auth decision made elsewhere. That last category is where the surprises live. A generic reader handles standard middleware well and hand-rolled auth badly; it reads explicit checkpoints well and inherited-elsewhere decisions worst of all — which is precisely where breaches actually breed.

A good curated skill doesn't pretend to omniscience — it narrates what it misses while it works. It flags that auth middleware on a parent router propagates to every route beneath it, so finding the handler but not where it's mounted under-reports protection. It flags implicit trust between internal services. It flags bespoke auth it reads worse than the standard kind. Then it closes with a calibrated self-grade — its own read of how much it caught, not a measured accuracy and not a benchmark about the move — and names where the last percents hide. A flagged uncertainty is more useful than a confident fabrication. Those flagged spots aren't noise to skim past; they're invitations addressed to you.

The skill reads standard middleware well and the weird bits badly. The weird bits are where the breaches live.

The upgrade · vibe-check → mapped surface

From threat-modelling a vibe to threat-modelling a map.

The upgrade is the same shape as every guardrail move: from something ephemeral and felt to something explicit and durable. On the left below, STRIDE walked against a remembered, hand-waved surface — "I think auth's fine" — six categories floating over nothing. That's the pub-quiz. On the right, STRIDE walked against an explicit six-surface map with the inherited-auth-bypass row lit up: defensible. Same six categories either way; the difference is entirely in whether they have a real surface to land on. The map is what turns a checklist into an analysis.

Process diagram · 02Same six categories, different ground. STRIDE over a vibe is a pub-quiz; STRIDE over a six-surface map is defensible.

Now the move you can't delegate. Read the map's confessed blind spots — don't skim them — and write the delta: two surfaces, each reasoned in this codebase's own terms. One the skill flagged harder than you would have (you'd under-weighted it). One you know matters that it missed — usually the part you'd call "the weird bit," where a generic pass runs thinnest. Push every reason until it names something a different codebase wouldn't share. "The billing webhook re-hits the queue on retry, so the same event gets reprocessed" beats "webhooks need auth." The first is yours; the second is anyone's.

And the delta — not the skill's wall of output — is what you ship to STRIDE next. The raw map is the input to your judgment; the delta is the artifact. Add a one-line context header if a teammate landing cold would miss something the map quietly assumes. That's the whole handoff: not a hardening backlog, not a fix list, just a map with your fingerprints on the surfaces only you knew to check. The loop below shows where this goes once it's a habit — each feature you map widens the kit the next feature's read starts from.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

The test is both shapes present, neither generic.

You know the move worked when the delta note has both shapes present and neither is generic — an over-weighted surface and a missed one, each reasoned in terms a different codebase wouldn't share. A teammate could hand it to STRIDE cold and threat-model something real, not a thin map. The tell that you skimmed: if the skill caught nothing you'd have missed, either the feature is trivial or you rushed to the next prompt and never sat with the map. And the failing case is sharp — if the only output is the skill's raw map with no delta layered on, the breadth read shipped and your read didn't, so STRIDE will chew on a map with no judgment in it. The percent the skill prints is its own calibrated read of its coverage — not a measured accuracy, not a benchmark about the pattern — and you treat it as a pointer to where to look, never as proof the map is right.

Why
The principle

Map what you're protecting before you name what could go wrong.

"The skill does the breadth you wouldn't have. You name the surface only you could."

The competence was never in knowing the threats — it's in knowing what you're protecting, and refusing to let a generic reader's confidence stand in for your codebase's particulars. A curated skill packages upstream work you don't re-derive: invoke, don't re-derive. But it reads standard middleware well and hand-rolled, inherited-elsewhere auth worst of all — exactly the surfaces where breaches breed. So the labour splits by what each party is good at: the subagent does breadth on a fresh context, cheap and calibrated-honest about its blind spots; you do the codebase-specific depth it structurally cannot have.

Mapping before threat-modelling is the same discipline as writing the failing test before the fix — ground the work in what's true before you reason about what could go wrong. The reason a CISO signs off isn't that you ran a tool. It's that the map has your fingerprints on the surfaces only you knew to check.

Breadth is borrowed. Judgment is yours.
Where it sits

This is the first guardrail in the trust arc: paired with STRIDE and the ADR under pressure, it walks a single engineer into staff-engineer and CISO review with a defensible map, a hardening decision, and a named residual risk — personal competence, made reviewable. And that same map-the-boundary-before-the-build discipline is what scope-for-trust raises to company scope, where it becomes the precondition for an organisation trusting any agent it didn't personally calibrate. Map a feature's access surface here; scope an agent's surface to what the org can trust there — same move, two surfaces.
