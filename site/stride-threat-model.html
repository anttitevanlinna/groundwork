Threat modeling before you build

Security... no different. If you just put together stuff and don't both analyse what you are doing and check that you did it right, then you end up with not-secure software. AI or not AI, the security work needs doing.

The principle: threat modelling is only useful if it ends in a decision — STRIDE is the surface to reject most things against, not an oracle you beg for the attacks you missed.

Run STRIDE like a field guide's identification key — not telling you what the threat is, but what it isn't, one fork at a time, until one name is left. In real terms: point a subagent at the feature you're shipping, take the access surface it returns, walk the six STRIDE categories against that map, reject most with a one-word reason, name the single worst realistic incident — then write the survivor down as an ADR your whole team inherits instead of re-argues.

From a subagent's surface map to one ADR. The rejected forks are the deliverable — not off-cuts.
The move, in one line

Read STRIDE the wrong way round and it's a finder — an oracle you point at code, hoping it surfaces the attacks you missed. Read it the right way round and it's a surface you reject against: you fire all six categories expecting to throw most of them out, until the one threat that survives is left holding the page with its alternatives already weighed. But STRIDE has nothing to chew on without a surface map. So the move starts one rung earlier than the threat list — it starts with what you feed the model.

Don't aim the six categories at the code in your head; that's pub-quiz threat-modelling, free-associated attacks with nothing to reject against. Instead, run the access-control read as a subagent — point vanilla Claude Code at this week's feature and let it return who-reaches-what, through which checkpoint, under which trust boundary. That returned map is the specimen. The descent below runs against one real one: a billing webhook that re-hits the queue on retry. Walk STRIDE against that, and every rejection means something, because each is a door on the map you looked at and chose to leave shut.

When two forks both say keep, the worst realistic incident breaks the tie — not the most exotic attack, the one that would actually wake someone at 3am. A retry storm that double-charges a customer is that one, so couplet 5 holds the page. If your kept fork doesn't match the worst-case story, you optimised for the cheapest fix, not the real risk. Re-run it.

The survivor isn't a finding — it's a decision you write down. Four sentences in docs/adr/NNNN-slug.md, or whatever convention your repo already uses for decisions. Context — the worst realistic case. Decision — make the handler idempotent on event-id at the edge. Alternatives considered — every reject above, transcribed with its one-word reason, because the rejected forks are the argument that the survivor beat them. Constraint — the queue is at-least-once, so dedupe at the edge rather than trust the queue to promise exactly-once. The rest of the STRIDE output stays alongside as evidence, not as a hardening backlog you've quietly committed yourself to.

Notice what the ADR is not. It sits on disk, discoverable and named — but unlike a CLAUDE.md rules file, it does not auto-load into every future session. It's read when someone explicitly reaches for it, which is exactly the right weight for a decision: heavy enough to inherit, light enough not to nag the context window of every unrelated run. That distinction is the whole craft here — guardrails that fire every time go in the rules file; decisions that someone consults on purpose go in the ADR folder.

A threat held up against the five it beat is an argument. A threat picked from thin air is a vibe.

One honesty clause rides in the leaf. STRIDE is a tool, not the law. Coined by Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg in a 1999 Microsoft threat-modelling memo and later sharpened by Adam Shostack in Threat Modeling: Designing for Security (2014), it's a mnemonic for six attack shapes — not a guarantee you caught yours. If the real risk is abuse-case or insider-threat shaped, STRIDE had no category for it: say so in Alternatives considered and reason in the lens that actually fit. The model gives you breadth fast; the codebase-specific judgement is yours to add, and it travels with the decision wherever it goes.

The compounding loop · the closing turn

Each ADR is the precedent the next threat-model starts from.

Here's why one defensible call outlives its single feature. Every engineer shipping an external surface carries one quiet unease — "I'm a bit nervous about that webhook." The trap isn't ignorance; you know roughly where the risk lives. The trap is that nobody made it a written decision, so the worry stays a private nerve: the staff engineer finds it in review, or prod finds it, and the team re-argues it from scratch on the next feature that touches the same surface. The write is the mechanism — a decision made under named pressure converts a private worry into shared, inheritable context. Map the surface, run STRIDE, reject most, write the ADR; then the loop closes onto the next feature, and the team's ADR folder is visibly thicker each pass.

One hardening decision is enough per feature; the team's breadth grows across many. The fourth time someone threat-models a surface like this billing webhook, they don't start from zero — they descend from the precedent the last engineer left. That's the difference between a security review that re-discovers the same threats every quarter and one that inherits them: the folder thickens, the re-arguing stops, and your CISO reads decisions instead of a backlog of TODOs.

How you know the threat was named · the test rides inline

A listed threat is not a named one.

The trap at the finish line is mistaking a long STRIDE list for a decision, so the test is falsifiable and runs on the ADR itself. First: the Context — or the scrollback — shows the worst-case story preceded the threat pick. Worst case first, then the choice; reversed order is menu-shopping and reads as reversed. Second: Alternatives considered names at least two rejected threats with a real reason each — acceptable risk, already mitigated, out of scope — not left dangling as backlog. Third, the sharpest tell: if the ADR could have been written without ever running the surface map, STRIDE was performative. The map's codebase-specific surfaces should be visible in the reasoning. A decision that reads like it came from a generic checklist probably did.

Monday move: pick one feature you're shipping this week with an external surface — a webhook, a public read, an action the agent now takes for a user. Vanilla Claude Code · access-control read as a subagent → STRIDE on what it surfaces · descend the six categories, rejecting most with a one-word reason · name the single worst realistic incident; the kept fork should match it · write the leaf in four sentences where your repo keeps decisions. The other threats stay alongside as evidence, not homework — and you arrive at review with the call already made.
