Name the threat before you build

The principle: threat modelling is only useful if it ends in a decision — STRIDE is the surface to reject most things against, not an oracle.

Before you harden anything, run STRIDE across a mapped surface, name the single worst realistic incident, pick the one threat that matches it, and write the call down as an ADR — so the worry becomes a defensible decision your whole team inherits instead of re-argues.

Fifteen rejected on purpose. The one you keep arrives with its alternatives already weighed.
The mechanism · what STRIDE actually chews on

STRIDE has nothing to chew on without a surface.

The most common way threat-modelling goes wrong is also the most innocent-looking: someone opens STRIDE and starts free-associating attacks against the code in their head. That's pub-quiz threat modelling — "STRIDE without an access-surface map is pub-quiz threat modeling." The six categories are a lens, not an oracle; a lens needs something to focus on. So the move starts one rung earlier: map the access surface first — run the access-control read as a subagent on the feature and you get a surface map, not a feeling. Who can reach what, through which checkpoint, under which trust boundary. Now STRIDE has a real object.

Then comes the part that feels backwards until you've done it once: you run all six categories expecting to reject most of them. Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information-disclosure, Denial-of-service, Elevation-of-privilege — fired at the mapped surface, the output has more entries than you want. That over-supply is the point. The structure exists so you can reject threats explicitly — acceptable risk, out of scope, already mitigated — which is exactly what makes the one you keep defensible. A threat held up against five it beat is an argument; a threat picked from thin air is a vibe. The diagram below shows the funnel: surface in, six lenses, many candidates, one decision out.

Process diagram · 01The surface feeds the six lenses; the lenses fan out into many candidates; the worst-realistic-incident story is the funnel that collapses them to one.

The funnel that collapses the candidates is not a scoring rubric — it's a story. Name the worst realistic incident, then the hardening decision is usually obvious. Not the most exotic attack; the one that would actually wake someone at 3am. For a billing webhook that re-hits the queue on retry, that story writes itself: a retry storm replays the event and double-charges a customer. Once the worst case is on the page, the threat you pick almost always falls out of it. And if your pick doesn't match the worst-case story, that's the tell — you optimised for the threat that's cheapest to fix, not the one that matters.

Pick one threat to harden, not five. If your pick doesn't match the worst-case story, you optimised for effort, not risk.

The upgrade · from a quiet worry to a written decision

The thing you're quietest about becomes the thing on the record.

Every engineer shipping a feature with an external surface carries one quiet unease — "I'm a bit nervous about that webhook." It's the bit you'd call "the weird bit": the endpoint trusting a header it shouldn't, the path that re-hits the queue on retry. The trap isn't ignorance — you know roughly where the risk lives. The trap is that nobody made the threat a written decision, so the staff engineer finds it in review, or the CISO finds it in prod, and the team re-argues it from scratch on the next feature that touches the same surface. The upgrade is to crystallise that unease into an ADR before the review, not after. Below: the faint thread of worry on the left, the durable artefact on the right.

Process diagram · 02The ADR is the artefact your CISO would actually read — one engineer briefing the next, not a reviewer's checkbox.

What you write down is deliberately small: one ADR per hardening decision, in whatever convention your repo already uses for decisions (default docs/adr/NNNN-slug.md). Four sections — Context, Decision, Alternatives considered, Constraint. The rejected STRIDE threats live in Alternatives considered with the reason each was rejected; the rest of the STRIDE output stays alongside as evidence, not as a hardening backlog you've quietly committed yourself to. And note what the ADR is not: it sits on disk, discoverable and named, but it does not auto-load into every future session the way a rules file does. It's read when someone explicitly reaches for it — which is exactly the right weight for a decision, heavy enough to inherit, light enough not to nag.

One more honesty clause: STRIDE is a tool, not the law. If its six categories were the wrong lens for this feature — if the real risk is abuse-case or insider-threat shaped — say so in Alternatives considered and reason in the lens that actually fit. The skill gives you breadth fast; the codebase-specific judgement is yours to add. A mnemonic that catches most attack shapes is not a guarantee you caught yours.

The compounding loop · one feature thickens the team's posture

Each ADR is the precedent the next threat-model starts from.

The diagram below shows why one defensible call is worth more than its single feature. Map the surface, run STRIDE, reject most, write the ADR — then the loop closes back onto the next feature's surface map, and the team's ADR folder is visibly thicker each pass. One hardening decision is enough per feature; the team kit's breadth grows across many features. The fourth time someone threat-models a surface like the billing webhook, they don't start from zero — they start from the precedent the last engineer left. That's the difference between a security review that re-discovers the same threats every quarter and one that inherits them.

What "Test" actually means · how you know the threat was named

A listed threat is not a named one.

The trap at the finish line is mistaking a long STRIDE list for a decision. So the test is falsifiable, and it runs on the artefact itself. First: the ADR's Context — or the scrollback — should show the worst-case story preceded the threat pick. Worst case first, then the choice. Reversed order is menu-shopping, and it reads as reversed. Second: Alternatives considered names at least two rejected threats with a real reason each — acceptable risk, already mitigated, out of scope — not left dangling as backlog. Third, the sharpest tell: if the ADR could have been written without ever running the surface map, STRIDE was performative. The map's codebase-specific surfaces should be visible in the reasoning. A decision that reads like it came from a generic checklist probably did.

Why
The principle

Threat modelling is only useful if it ends in a decision. STRIDE is the surface you reject against — not an oracle.

"The ADR is the one thing your CISO will actually read."

STRIDE's real gift isn't catching everything — it's giving you a structured surface to reject most things against, so the one threat you harden arrives with its alternatives already weighed. That's the difference between a defensible architectural call and a vibe. The mechanism is the write: a decision made under named pressure converts a private worry into shared, inheritable context. Most teams skip the write — so the same threat gets re-argued on every feature that touches the same surface.

The name is honest about its limits. STRIDE — coined by Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg in a 1999 Microsoft threat-modelling memo, later sharpened by Adam Shostack in Threat Modeling: Designing for Security (2014) — is a mnemonic for the six ways a system gets attacked, not a guarantee you caught yours. A threat you haven't named is one the whole team re-discovers, one expensive review at a time. Naming it once, in writing, is the cheapest insurance in the building.

A listed threat is a chore. A named one is a decision.
