The mechanism · why the rubric is the weakest possible authority

The rubric you trusted most is the one you measured least.

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of pre-written rubrics: you write the check list before you know what the agent will get wrong. The rubric is calibrated to your expectations, not to the agent's failure modes. It passes everything it was never designed to catch — and you will not discover this until a claim slips through and someone downstream notices. By which point the authority you thought you'd built is borrowed time.

The move that fixes this is empirical rather than authoritative. Pull the actual claims the agent produced. Adjudicate them yourself — grounded, overreach, or fabricated — against the sources you have. Now you are the ground truth. Run several candidate detectors against that same pool, each writing results to its own file, none blended. Read the scoreboard: one detector won on precision and coverage; another had better recall but missed half the overreach cases. The data names the winner. That is a judge you can defend.

The default in Agents 101 Module 5 starts with thirty claims — enough to prove the method on your own output. (Illustrative default — production judges scale to hundreds of claims and running corrections; the method is fixed, the benchmark size is not.) The size is not the point; adjudicating those claims yourself is. You cannot benchmark a judge you haven't run on real output you actually produced.

The crossing move · from opinion to infrastructure

The benchmark is what separates a decision from a guess.

There is a category error that survives most AI pilots: using a rubric as if it were a test. A rubric tells you what good looks like in advance; a benchmark tells you whether your detector finds good in practice. These feel similar from the outside — both involve a list and a tick — but their epistemic weight is completely different. The rubric's authority is the writer; the scoreboard's authority is the data. One of these survives a pointed question from a sceptical stakeholder; the other doesn't.

The crossing move is the benchmark itself — not a new tool, not a platform. You already have the output. You already have the sources. Twenty minutes adjudicating ten claims builds the ground truth half. From that moment you can run a candidate detector and score it. Nothing required that isn't in a plain text editor and the output you produced yesterday.

⚠ Two honest labels, stated once. The hero scoreboard is an illustrative mock — its precision and recall are placeholders for shape, not figures from a measured run; the only real numbers are the ones you produce on your own output. And "30 claims" is a pedagogically calibrated default from Agents 101 Module 5, not a validated production minimum — production judges scale to hundreds of claims and running corrections. The method is fixed; both the numbers and the pool size are yours to fill in.

There is a side effect worth naming. Running the benchmark teaches you what the agent actually gets wrong — claim by claim, not in aggregate. The overreach cases are more instructive than the fabrications; the fabrications are obvious, the overreach is where a defensible agent walks into indefensible territory without either party noticing. That knowledge is structural. It flows back into the guardrail file, the context you provide, the constraints you place on the next generation pass. The benchmark is not a one-time quality gate. It's a diagnostic that sharpens the upstream build.

The scoreboard also teaches you to evaluate evaluators — and that move transfers. You bench-tested groundedness today. Tomorrow it's tone. Next month it's brand compliance or regulatory language. The specific domain is incidental; the empirical method is permanent. Every future quality question you automate follows the same recipe. That is why this pattern sits at a gate, not a tip.

What "Test" actually means · the scoreboard read

A colleague reads the scoreboard and explains why this judge won.

The test for a benchmarked judge is not the precision number. The number can be right by chance. The test is whether a colleague — one who wasn't in the room when you ran it — can read the scoreboard and say: here is why detector 1 won, here is the claim shape detector 3 missed, here is what the known limit line covers. If they can, the authority has transferred. If they can't, the judge is not a judge; it's a number with a label.

The known limit line is not an admission of failure. It is the most important line in the file. Every detector has a reach boundary; the judge that names its boundary is the one that doesn't fail silently. A narrow judge that works beats a broad judge that pretends — because the broad judge fails on the exact claim shapes the team assumes it caught. Name the limit once, written down, and the team can reason around it.

Use when

Quality matters enough that "looks good" is not a decision method — and someone will eventually ask: how do you know the judge works? Recurs after any first agent build producing text with downstream consequences, at the moment a team considers trusting agent output for external audiences, whenever the output sits at uneasy distance from the sources.

Build

A 30-claim benchmark (illustrative default — scale in production): evidence roster + claim pool + adjudicated verdicts. A detector scoreboard. A judge file with the winning detector configured and a "Known limit:" line that names what it cannot reach.

Test

A colleague reads the scoreboard and explains why this judge won and where it stops — without asking you.

Core moves

Five moves that earn the judge its row

Quality authority shifts from trust to evidence in five moves.
1

Pull claims from the real output

Not a hypothetical. The actual thing. Thirty claims is the default — illustrative; scale in production.

2

Adjudicate against sources yourself

Grounded / overreach / fabricated. You are the ground truth. This is what makes everything downstream defensible.

3

Run candidate detectors blind

Each writes to its own file. No blending before scoring. Four is a good starting count.

4

Score precision, recall, coverage

Read the scoreboard row by row. The data names the winner — not the method's reputation.

5

Package winner as a judge file

Configured detector plus a "Known limit:" line naming what it cannot catch. A narrow judge that works beats a broad one that pretends.

Then the judge is infrastructure, not a one-time check. Every eval pass makes the next benchmark's claim pool sharper. The specific domain changes; the empirical method stays.

Fastest entry point: any output you already have. Pull ten claims (not thirty — just ten). Open the sources. Adjudicate each one. Plain text file · agent prompts for detectors · markdown for the judge · ~20 min. You've built the ground truth half. Everything else follows.

Why
The principle

Empirical method selection over authority. The scoreboard is the source of trust, not the method's pedigree.

A judge you benchmarked beats a rubric you trusted in advance.

The rubric you trust most in advance is the weakest possible quality authority — you calibrated it to your expectations before the agent ran, before you knew which claim shapes it would miss. The benchmark flips that entirely. You pull real claims from real output, adjudicate them yourself, and the data names the winner. You walk out with a judge you'd defend in a meeting, not a document you'd apologise for.

There is a subtler transfer: running the benchmark teaches you to evaluate evaluators — and that move applies to tone, brand, compliance, any quality dimension you'll ever automate. The domain is groundedness. The skill is empirical method selection. Every future quality question in the operating model follows the same recipe.

This is also the personal quality gate: the moment you stop trusting agent outputs and start measuring them. Without it, every downstream quality claim at team or company level rests on opinion. With it, the authority is owned, transferable, and defensible — which is what makes the next autonomy rung earnable rather than asserted.

The method you measured beats the method you read about by the same margin the pilot beats the press release.
Test in practice

Hand the scoreboard to a colleague who wasn't in the room. Ask them: why did detector 1 win? Where does it stop? If they can answer — from the file, not from memory — the judge is earned.

If they say "it scored highest" without explaining the claim shapes it missed and the ones it caught, the scoreboard isn't readable enough yet. That's the edit: make the recall gap visible in the table, not just in the number. A readable scoreboard is part of the artefact.
