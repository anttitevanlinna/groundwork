Name the three blocks

The principle: recognition before naming — a frame is only trustworthy once you've watched it fit your own mess.

Sort the context you already keep — rules, decisions, quality bars — into three named kinds, and let the slot that comes up empty tell you what the agent is missing. You're not authoring; you're auditing a pile you already built.

The lines didn't change. The empty lane did — and an empty lane is what the agent never inherited.
The mechanism · why a sort changes nothing and reveals everything

The lines don't change. The slots do.

You have a CLAUDE.md, probably a CLAUDE.local.md, a few observations jotted down, an ADR or two, a skill you wrote last week. Each was written in the moment, for its own reason, and nobody ever stepped back to ask what kind of thing each one is. So when a real task lands and you go to walk your material against it, you're reading a flat list with no shape — and a flat list is exactly the surface that hides its own holes. Presence is easy to see; absence is invisible. You can stare at twenty good lines and feel well-prepared, because the missing twenty-first leaves no mark on the page.

The move is borrowed, and it travels with its source attached: Huryn's three-block memory — a frame for naming what you've built, not a template to fill in. Sort every line of context into one of three kinds. Block 1 is observation → rule: the conditioned reflexes you've encoded — we use Postgres, not Mongo; webhook signatures get validated before dispatch. Block 2 is decisions plus the alternatives you rejected — the choice, the road not taken, the constraint that forced it. Block 3 is quality criteria — what "done" looks like, what you'd reject the output for. Same lines, three labelled slots. Below: the identical pile, before and after the sort.

Process diagram · 01The lines didn't change — the slots did, and an empty slot is a finding. Not new content; new legibility.

Here's the counterintuitive part, and it's the whole reason the move is worth five minutes: the value isn't in the rules you've written — it's in the category that's empty. A sort doesn't add a single line. What it adds is a structure with named slots, so that one slot sitting bare becomes impossible to un-see. That's the cognitive trick the frame plays — it converts an absence (no quality criteria) into a presence (an empty Block 3 staring back at you). The bare lane is louder than every full one. You can argue with a rule; you can't argue with a slot that has nothing in it.

A flat list flatters the pile. The empty block tells you the truth.

Two slots tend to come up short, and each absence costs the agent something specific. A thin Block 2 means decisions were made and then forgotten — the agent inherits the what but not the why, so it cheerfully proposes the alternative you already rejected for a reason it never learned. A decision recorded with no rejected alternative is itself a finding: a Block 2 entry missing half its content. And Block 3 is, in practice, the emptiest of the three — not because it's a measured rate, but because most engineers carry their quality bar in their head and never wrote it down, so the agent never inherits the taste that would let it reject its own bad output. The sort doesn't fix this. It just makes the hole stop hiding.

The upgrade move · template-filled → recognition-audited

Point the lens at your own mess, never a blank template.

There are two ways to arrive at a three-block memory, and only one survives contact with a task. The tempting path starts from a blank template: open three empty headings and dutifully author rules until each block looks full. It produces a tidy, complete-looking system that was never tested against real work — context that reads well and means nothing, because you wrote what you imagined your standards were, not what your repo has actually been enforcing. The honest path runs backwards: point the frame at the material you already wrote and classify what's there. Recognition, not authoring. The frame earns its name after you've matched a real line to a block — never before. Below: both paths, and the one that holds.

Process diagram · 02Authoring from a blank template yields tidy context never tested against work. Recognising the blocks in lived material yields an honest audit — gaps and all.

The frame is permanent; the contents compound. The first sort is a snapshot — it tells you which kind of context is load-bearing today and which is missing. But the real return comes from running the loop: a task lands, you sort, the thinnest block surfaces, you fill the worst gap by routing it to its proper home, you send the work off, and the return sharpens the blocks for the next pass. The three lanes are fixed; the lines inside them grow. Each lap the bare lane carries one more line, and the next task starts against a richer map than the last. Boring on the first lap; decisive by the tenth.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

The frame has to fit your own ADR — not a textbook example.

The sort worked if you can point at any line in your context and say which block it is without hesitating. A line that resists classification isn't a mystery third category — it's either redundant, or it's quietly doing two jobs and should be split. The harder test is the honest one: at least one block should come up thin or empty, and you should be able to name what that absence costs the agent on the very next task. A sort where all three lanes look full means one of two things — you have unusually mature context, or you're flattering the pile. When in doubt, re-check Block 3; it's the easiest to fool yourself about. And the negative test that catches the most common failure: if you could only illustrate the blocks with invented examples and not one line of your own, the sort hasn't happened. You've learned a vocabulary, not audited a system. The proof is recognising your last real ADR as a Block 2 entry, not nodding along to a clean definition.

Use when

You've accumulated rules, memory, ADRs and skills and you can't tell whether you've built a system or a heap — typically right before you send a real slice off to an agent and want to know the context is load-bearing.
