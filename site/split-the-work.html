Split the work across agents

The principle: the filesystem is the meeting room — parallel agents coordinate through files you can inspect, not a chat that smooths the friction away.

Break a question into agents only when one agent genuinely can't be one — when the access, dialect, or stance the work needs truly differ — then make them disagree on disk and force a framework to pick. SCOPE · personal → team → company: a single builder's research habit becomes the org's standing knowledge surface.

Three honest seams a single summary would have erased. The filesystem is the meeting room.
The mechanism · what the split actually buys

More agents isn't more intelligence.

Multi-agent has a glamour problem. It feels powerful — three minds, a swarm, an org chart of bots — and most builders reach for it because the shape looks serious, not because the work demanded it. Here's the unglamorous truth: a second agent that reads the same sources, speaks the same dialect, and holds the same stance as the first is not a colleague. It's overhead wearing a costume. You've doubled the coordination cost and bought nothing, because anything the pair could conclude, one good prompt could have concluded alone.

So the move starts with a refusal. Before you split anything, name the forcing axis: is it access (the agents literally reach different things — one sees the wiki, one the warehouse, one the open web), dialect (the sources don't speak the same language and shouldn't be normalised into one before you've looked), or stance (the answer needs a planner, an assumption-tester, and a reframer arguing, not one summary splitting the difference)? If you can't name which of the three is making one agent impossible, you don't have a split. You have one prompt cosplaying as three. Sizing a market is the canonical case, because the answer is genuinely three answers in three dialects. There's the loud answer — press releases, analyst predictions, the numbers everyone repeats. There's the quiet answer — practitioner blogs, GitHub activity, what people say at conferences when the slides are off. And there's the academic answer — benchmarks, papers, the careful caveated version. Normalise those into one tidy figure before you've looked and you've thrown away exactly the disagreement that tells you what's real. Three retrievers, each fluent in one source's language, return non-comparable outputs by design. Don't let any of them average on the way in.

Faking the multi-agent shape with one prompt wearing three hats is the tell that you didn't need to split.

The upgrade · from a prompt in three hats to agents on disk

From the fake split to the split that bears load.

The brittle version is one chat thread doing all of it: a single agent prompted to "consider the market data, the practitioner view, and the academic literature" — three hats, one head, one transcript that quietly reconciles everything as it goes. It looks like multi-agent. It behaves like a summary. The load-bearing version gives each dialect its own agent and its own file — parallel sessions on a shared folder, or subagents inside one session; different shapes, same territory — and then makes a fourth move do the work the others can't: a framework that forces the question "where do these disagree?" before a single sentence of synthesis. Below: three hats writing to one chat on the left, three agents writing to three files on the right.

Process diagram · 02One head in three hats reconciles; three agents on three files, under a framework, are forced to show where they disagree.

A word on that framework, because it's the step most people skip and the one that decides whether the whole thing was worth the coordination cost. A synthesiser left un-prompted does what any model does at a fork: it rounds the corner and makes peace. Hand it three conflicting files and no spine, and it will produce a fluent, reasonable, utterly beige reconciliation — the very thing you split to avoid. Give it a framework — a strategy kernel by default, swap a stance if your challenge fits another (the default trio below is a starting point, not the only correct configuration) — and it stops averaging and starts picking. The framework is the synthesiser's spine. Without one, you've built an expensive way to get the single-agent answer back.

The compounding loop · what changes between passes

Next split starts from earned rules, not defaults.

The first time you split a question, you're guessing at the division of labour. By the third time, you shouldn't be. The artefact that carries the learning forward is a root ./CLAUDE.md that governs how the agents divide the work — and in the review after each run, you sharpen it from the four agent prompts and the retrieval files: this zone was noise, that stance was redundant, the framework needed a sharper "show me the seam" instruction. Each pass the rules file gets visibly richer, and the next question's split starts from earned division-of-work rules instead of a blank guess. What "Test" actually means · in practice

The split earned its keep if the seam shows.

Here's the test, and it's harsher than "looks thorough." The answer has to be specific enough to bring forward and its uncertainty has to be plain enough to inspect — and a reader has to be able to see where the agents disagreed, because the synthesiser picked a direction instead of averaging the three into a hedge. Then the negative test, which most work fails — and that failing is the point. If you can't name which of access, dialect, or stance forced the split, or if one good prompt would have produced the same answer, you didn't need to split. You added coordination cost for nothing. Most work lives on that side of the line; the discipline is learning the move and respecting the default.

Use when

One agent can't sensibly cover the access, dialect, or stance the work needs — and you can name which of the three is forcing your hand. Can't name one? Keep the single agent.

Build

Why
The principle

The filesystem is the meeting room. Parallel minds coordinate through files you can inspect — not a chat that smooths the friction away.

"Three agents don't make you smarter. Three different kinds of access, dialect, or stance do — and if you can't name which, you've hired three of the same agent."

More agents is not more intelligence; it's more access, more dialect, more stance — and only when those genuinely differ. The counterintuitive part is that the move which earns its keep is most often the refusal to split: most builders reach for multi-agent because it feels powerful, and most of the time one good prompt would have done it cheaper. When the split is real, the danger moves to the seams — conflicts get dropped, dialects clash, the synthesiser rounds everything into beige. The whole skill is watching where the pieces meet.

And the synthesiser needs a framework precisely because, left alone, a model's default at a disagreement is to round the corner and make peace. A framework forces it to choose. That is why the coordination happens on disk and not in a chat: a chat thread is built to reconcile, and reconciliation is exactly the friction you most need to see.

Most work doesn't need the split. Learn the move; respect the default.
