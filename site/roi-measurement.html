Take the slide apart backwards.

The principle: you cannot take apart a number that was never put together — a confident ROI figure almost always rests on a baseline nobody recorded, so the honest move is to run the slide's autopsy before you trust its verdict.

Someone shows you the slide: "AI is delivering a 340% return." Don't argue with the number — unwind it, last step first, the way a magician's trick collapses once you're told where the coin went. By the bottom you'll hit the missing baseline, and know what the bar chart was built to hide: there is no validated institutional-ROI number yet, and the dishonesty was load-bearing.

Someone just showed you the slide · the situation

"AI is delivering a 340% return." Don't argue with the number.

Clean bar, confident decimal, a logo in the corner. Maybe a vendor presented it. Maybe a peer said it over dinner. Maybe it was your own deck and you half-believed it. This is the moment the card is for: you can feel the AI working — people are faster, the demos land — and yet a single bright percentage is standing in for a return nobody has actually measured. Arguing the number is a trap; haggle it down to "only" 180% and you've conceded the frame, that a clean figure exists to be negotiated. It doesn't. So don't argue. Take it apart backwards — start at the answer and unwind it one assumption at a time, the way a magician's trick collapses the instant you're told where the coin actually went. By the time you reach the bottom you'll know something the slide was built to hide.

A consultancy sells you the slide. The move is to take it apart in public — and refuse to hand you a prettier one.

One honesty up front, because the whole autopsy depends on it: 340% is a stand-in, an imagined slide standing for the genre. There is no real measured figure here — that is precisely the point. The numbers that are real arrive later, and every one of them measures the absence of the return, never its size.

Unwind it, last step first · the core move

The 340% is a quotient. Peel the saving off the top.

Where did the saving come from? Hours saved. Push: whose hours, counted how? Almost always the answer is a self-reported survey — "how much time do you reckon the assistant saves you?" — multiplied across the org. Set that beside the one place someone ran it properly. The UK's GOV.UK controlled trial of Microsoft 365 Copilot — roughly a thousand civil servants doing office work, not coding — measured zero productivity improvement. A controlled trial, not a survey. The hours on your slide are the hours people felt; the hours in the trial are the hours that moved. They are not the same quantity, and only one of them shows up in a P&L.

Keep going. Even granting the felt hours, the slide turned them into money with a loaded salary rate — fully-burdened cost per hour, as though every minute reclaimed gets re-spent on equally valuable work. It doesn't. Faros AI looked across roughly ten thousand developers and found zero company-level correlation between AI adoption and output. Time can be saved at the desk and vanish before it reaches the ledger. The salary multiplier assumes a conversion the data says didn't happen.

Now the quietest step, the one that does the most work: annualisation. A two-week pilot's saving, times twenty-six, becomes a year. NBER's economic research found 89% of managers report no change — and managers are exactly the people whose teams those annualised pilots roll up into. You're projecting a line that, at the scale where it would have to show, the people running it can't see.

You've stripped the saving down to felt hours, falsely converted and falsely extended. One step left — and it's the foundation.

The step that collapses it · the baseline that was never taken

To claim a return you must know the "before." Nobody took it.

Stop and ask when anyone measured the before-state — the real cycle time, the real cost-to-serve, before the tool arrived. They didn't. And here's why it isn't an oversight: the playbook for turning a traditional organisation AI-native does not exist yet. There is no settled method, which means there is no clean baseline, which means there is no validated ROI number for institutional AI today — not on this slide, not on yours, not anywhere. Open frontier — no validated method exists today; the transition playbook does not yet exist. The decimal point was punctuation pretending to be precision.

Seen from the inside, then, the trick is this: the slide manufactured the experience of measurement and skipped the substance. The vendor's usage metric — logins, prompts, percentage of staff active — does the same job from the other end. It always rises, because people do log in, and rising feels like return. Both give you the sensation of a scoreboard with nothing behind the glass. The practitioner Will Manidis named the family of these in early 2026 — a "tool-shaped object," software that produces the experience of productivity without the substance: "the primary product of that spending is the experience of spending it." A fabricated ROI percentage is the tool-shaped object one level up.

Process diagram · 02When you unwound the saving, the cost side stayed solid. So the honest restatement isn't "no return" — it's a definite cost against a return nobody has earned the right to name yet.

That asymmetry is the whole situation, and it's the one thing the bar chart was designed to keep you from seeing. Which means the deliverable of the autopsy is not a corrected percentage — chasing a "truer" number just rebuilds the lie with better arithmetic. The deliverable is the empty space, named out loud, plus the honest proxies you can read while the baseline gets built. There are three worth watching, and none of them is a return figure.

First, the amplifier read: did the underlying system get healthier, or just faster at being what it already was? A tool sits on top of the system you already run — it does not repair it — so a "return" booked on an unrepaired system is a baseline error waiting to surface. (Its neighbour, redesign what the AI revealed, takes that amplifier apart in full; here it is only the first thing the autopsy checks.) Second, the workflow-redesign signal, the proxy with the most behind it: Stanford's read across 51 enterprise deployments found the one factor separating orgs that captured institutional value was whether the workflow itself changed — roles, hand-offs, decisions — with high performers roughly three times more likely to have redesigned, and no org capturing value with zero organizational change. Third, the absorption rate — how much of your real work the system has actually reached; today's agents touch only a thin slice, around 20% of enterprise data, so a rising slice is honest evidence the value is institutionalizing rather than visiting one demo. Each is a proxy — a directional read, not a validated return figure. What goes after the word "instead" in your sentence is the start of an actual baseline.

Tonight's move · run the autopsy out loud

The deliverable is a sentence you can both say without flinching.

Take the most confident AI-return claim near you — the vendor's, the board's, your own — and walk it backwards in front of one trusted colleague, naming each manufacturing step as you pull it: here's the felt-hours survey standing in for measured hours; here's the salary rate assuming saved time became valuable time; here's the annualisation; here's the baseline that was never taken. You're not trying to land on a corrected percentage. You're proving, out loud, that the number can't yet be honestly built. No platform, no budget, no sign-off — the autopsy runs on a claim you already have and a colleague who already knows the work.

"We are spending real money on a return we have not yet earned the right to measure — so here is what we'll watch instead."

That sentence is the deliverable of the evening, and what goes after "instead" is the start of an actual baseline — the amplifier read, the redesign question, the absorption rate, whichever you can take tonight. The bravery is stopping there, at the empty space, instead of papering it with a decimal. And note where the move lands you in the bigger picture: this is the honest ledger that scaling rests on. The proxy you read tonight is what a builder-leader carries to the board in place of a vanity dashboard — it reframes the conversation from "are people using it" to "is the institution getting healthier," which is the conversation that licenses real redesign budget at company scale.

Why
The principle

No recorded baseline, no honest return — the individual high is real, but it was never measured against the institution it was meant to move.

"The decimal point was punctuation pretending to be precision."

Anything that measures the individual high — felt hours, logins, a survey multiplied across the org — will always overstate the business return, because the high is real and it just doesn't aggregate. A research synthesis across more than fifteen independent sources keeps hitting the same wall: Stanford, the controlled GOV.UK Copilot trial, NBER, Goldman, Faros AI across ten thousand developers, all converging on individual productivity does not roll up into institutional value on its own. The mechanism is mundane and total — a tool sits on top of an existing system and amplifies it; healthy system, it compounds; broken system, it fails faster. Klarna ran exactly that arc, subtracting hard and then reversing.

And the reason the institutional return resists measurement isn't laziness — it's that the playbook for turning a traditional org AI-native does not exist yet, so there is no validated baseline to measure return against. That competence — to dismantle a fabricated number on sight and refuse to rebuild it prettier — doesn't transfer by being understood. It transfers by being done, once, out loud, on a real claim. And then it's yours: the reader who can take a slide apart never has to buy one again.

Don't chase a truer number — that just rebuilds the lie with better arithmetic.
