The fixed-judge loop

The principle: a yardstick you rewrite is not a yardstick — freeze the standard and the work will chase it.

Stop reviewing every output by hand. Freeze the judge you already trust, let a separate generator chase it round after round, and feed every correction into the tactic file — never the judge. The standard holds still so the work can move; that's how quality stops being one desk and becomes infrastructure a whole team runs unattended.

Only one file is allowed to move. The tactic chases; the judge holds.
The mechanism · why a later score means anything at all

Move the yardstick and you've measured nothing.

Here is the trap nearly every leader walks into, because it feels like diligence. You set out to improve a recurring output, so each round you tweak the work — and, while you're at it, you tweak the standard too. A sharper rubric here, a kinder threshold there. Round three looks better than round one, and you congratulate yourself. But you've learned nothing: the work and the yardstick both moved, so you cannot tell whether the work got better or the bar got softer. A score is only comparable when the thing producing it stays fixed. That single fact is the whole pattern. Freeze the judge, and round three's number finally has something to say about round one.

Notice this is the exact inverse of how a fresh agent gets steered. In make output yours, you change the input and the output drifts to follow — same words, different answer. Here you do the opposite: you pin the standard so the output can't hide behind a moving target, and let the generation drift toward it. One file is allowed to move; one is not. The skill is the discipline to keep them straight under the pressure to "just nudge the rubric" when a round disappoints.

Process diagram · 01The inverse of "context shifted." Here the standard is pinned and the work moves toward it — a later round is comparable to an earlier one only because the judge held still.

One structural rule makes the whole thing honest: the agent that writes the work and the agent that grades it must be different. A generator that marks its own homework drifts, slowly and plausibly, toward flattering itself — not out of malice, but because the cheapest way to score higher is to soften what counts as good. The asymmetry is the design: one part writes and wants to pass, one part rejects and owes nothing. Keep them in separate sessions, separate files, and the judge stays a judge instead of quietly becoming the generator's press secretary.

Freezing the judge looks like giving up control. It is the opposite: it's your taste, delegated and made durable.

The upgrade · from your desk to a loop that runs without you

From reading every draft to designing the loop that reads them.

The reason this matters to a busy leader is brutally practical. Today the recurring check — the weekly board narrative, the customer release blurb, the renewal-risk read — runs because you run it. You are the bottleneck and the quality bar at once, and the day you're on a plane the bar travels with you. The upgrade is to lift your standard out of your head and into a frozen judge, so a generator can chase it while you do something else entirely. You stop reviewing every output; you start designing the loop that reviews them. That is the move from operator to owner, and it costs nothing but the restraint to leave the judge alone.

The compounding loop · what is allowed to thicken

The tactic gets thicker; the judge stays a stone.

The loop has four beats — generate, judge, rewrite the tactic, re-run — and the whole craft is in which node grows. Each round you read what the judge flagged and write the catch into the tactic file, never the judge. The tactic is the generator's how-to; it accretes every correction and visibly thickens pass over pass. The judge accretes nothing. Picture it as the diagram below: one node fattening with each loop, one node sitting there like a stone. And keep the round-by-round trail — the score, what the judge kept catching, what the tactic never managed to absorb. That trail is your evidence the loop works, not your faith that it does — and it's also what you'll hand the room when you ask to make this an always-on team check.

What "Test" actually means · the question that catches self-flattery

The test isn't a better score — it's a trail you can read.

You know the loop worked when the later output is better by the same yardstick and you can point at the score trail rather than your gut — and when the tactic diffs read like a learning record a colleague could follow. But the test that earns its keep is the one that catches the loop quietly cheating. Ask it: "which rule did you claim to remove but actually kept under another name? Quote both lines." If generation could secretly edit the judge, that question has nowhere honest to land. And the negative test is blunt: if your "improvement" came from loosening the judge mid-run, you didn't tighten the work — you moved the goalposts. A rewritten yardstick is not a yardstick.

Use when

The same check has to run again and again — a weekly board note, a customer release blurb, a renewal-risk score — and you're the bottleneck running it. You've already benchmarked a judge you trust; now the work needs to keep clearing that bar without you reading every draft.

Build

A re-runnable loop with three separated parts: a frozen judge, a separate generator, and an evolving tactic file — plus the inspectable round trail of scores and tactic diffs.

Then it's infrastructure, not a chore: the work sharpens because the standard doesn't. Each round leaves the tactic richer and the judge untouched — the verification primitive you reuse on every recurring output.

Monday move: pick one output you sanity-check yourself. Write its standard as a one-page judge prompt and resolve never to edit it this run. Three markdown files · two separate agent sessions · no platform · no budget · no sign-off · ~30 min. Run the draft twice, feeding only the judge's flags into a tactic note. If round two beats round one against the unchanged judge, you've felt the loop — and you arrive at the team-check conversation with a score trail, not a pitch.

Why
The principle

A yardstick you rewrite is not a yardstick. You move up a level by refusing to move the standard.

"Freeze the standard and the work will chase it. Move the standard and you've learned nothing."

The counterintuitive bit a leader feels in the gut: freezing the judge looks like surrendering control, and is the opposite. The frozen judge is your taste, delegated and made durable — so the generator can chase it while you do something else, and the self-improving generator becomes the work you no longer do by hand. The discipline that makes it real is the separation: a generator that grades itself drifts toward flattering itself, so the asymmetry — one part writes, one part rejects — is the whole design.

This is the same shape the software lane calls verifier-shaped-to-failure: freeze the check, evolve the code. Different surface, identical move — pin the standard, let the work chase it. Verification is where autonomy is earned, and a standard that holds still is what makes the chasing mean anything.

The work moves. The judge doesn't. That's the integrity of the loop.
Where this sits · personal → team → company

A fixed judge is the verification primitive a company's autonomy rests on. Alone it raises one person off the bottleneck; shared, it lets a team run the same standard unattended; and once your taste lives in a frozen judge that separate agents are scored against, quality stops being a person reviewing every output and becomes infrastructure the whole company can trust agent work against — one standard, not one desk at a time. That trust is the precondition everything above it composes on.

Test · in practice

For two-audience work the two-register output shape plugs straight in: the same content in two registers — an engineer changelog and a customer announce, or a board narrative and an ops dashboard — held under one fixed editorial judge so the registers don't drift apart. (One shape, the way a cookbook names a dish — not a measured result.) The integrity question still applies: ask the loop which rule it claimed to drop but kept under another name, and make it quote both lines.
