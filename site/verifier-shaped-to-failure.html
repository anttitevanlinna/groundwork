The principle: a verifier's authority comes from fit — from firing on candid evidence, not from how much it checks.

After a long run goes wrong, name the one failure that cost the most — then build the one verifier whose shape catches that failure, not the check that's easiest to reach for. The verifier you fit by hand is your first eval, and the seed every team-scale eval grows from.

The failure picks the verifier. Not your comfort — the dashed thread is the check that fires green and sees nothing.
The scar · a gate that fired green and saw nothing

We built a gate to catch fabrication. It passed a fabrication.

We learned this one on our own keyboard. Building this very field guide, a render agent produced one of the cards and its coherence gate lit green — theme intact, structure clean, every neighbour link present. The card went into the queue with a PASS. Except two of its "connects-to" neighbour links pointed at cards that don't exist — slugs the agent had confabulated, plausible-but-fake, that would have 404'd the moment anyone clicked them. Caught one gate past where they should have been, before anything shipped. The gate had done exactly what we wrote it to do: it checked that every link was present. It never checked that any link resolved. We had shaped the verifier to the wrong failure.

That is the whole pattern in one wound. A green light fired on a fabrication because it was never shaped to the failure we actually feared — fake neighbours, not missing ones. So here is the moment this pattern lives in: a long run comes back, you read the artefact, and you name where it went wrong. The agent quietly changed what it was solving (drift), or lost the thread across a long session (rot), or shipped something that looks right and isn't (plausible-but-wrong). Diagnosis done. Now you reach for a check — and the trap springs. You reach for the comfortable check, the one your hands already know, not the one that fits the failure you just named. A shell-hook for a qualitative miss. An LLM-judge for something a one-line lint would catch deterministically. Both fire green. Both catch nothing.

The counterintuitive part is what makes a verifier strong. It isn't rigour — it's fit. A meticulous shell-hook is worthless against a drift it cannot see; a careful judge is overkill, and noise, on a thing an exit code would have flagged for free. A verifier you didn't shape to a named failure is a green light wired to nothing. It tells you everything is fine in exactly the cases where it can't tell. The check that protects you is the one whose shape matches the failure that actually happened — read from your own artefact, not chosen from a menu in the abstract.

A green light wired to nothing is worse than no light. It tells you it's fine in exactly the case it can't see.

Process diagram · 01Same input, three verdicts. Only the verifier shaped to the failure fires red — the others pass it cleanly and tell you nothing.

Why three shapes and not one universal check? Because the three failures are genuinely distinct, and no single instrument sees all three. Deterministic-wrong — it doesn't compile, the lint fails, the bytes don't match — wants a shell-hook: an exit code can see it. Qualitative-wrong — it reads wrong, asserts something subtly off, breaks a contract no test names — wants an LLM-judge: only a reading sees it. And goal drift — the run wandered off its own intent — wants a Ralph re-feed: only re-grounding the agent in what it set out to do fixes it. One move per failure mode, not three to memorise. You pick the shape against the diagnosis, not the path of least resistance.

Notice the discipline this asks of you: finish the diagnosis before you reach for the fix. The reflex is to jump from "it failed" straight to "I'll add a check" — and that jump is exactly how the wrong instrument gets built. The question matters more than the answer: what validation would have caught this in minutes, not hours? Answer that honestly and the shape falls out of the failure. Skip it, and you'll armour-plate the one wall the attacker never came at.

Convergent practitioner pattern · not a vendor menu

The three failure modes (drift · rot · plausible-but-wrong) and the three matched shapes (shell-hook · LLM-judge · re-feed) are a convergent practitioner pattern — Ronacher's three-pattern and Cherny's three stop-hook shapes — not an Anthropic-documented feature or an official Claude Code taxonomy. Treat it as the three shapes practitioners converge on, open to a fourth, not a fixed list handed down by the tool.

The upgrade move · reflexive check → failure-fitted eval

From a check that names nothing to one that names its own target.

The move that turns a reflex into an instrument is small and almost clerical: make the verifier name its own target in its first lines. Line one states the dominant failure it's built to catch. Line two names the shape that catches it — hook, judge, or re-feed. A check whose intent isn't readable is a check nobody trusts and nobody reuses; it rots into a green tick everyone clicks past. When the first ten lines say what failure this guards against, a teammate reading it cold knows what it covers — and so does future-you, three weeks on, when you'd otherwise have forgotten why it exists.

Process diagram · 02Reflex → instrument. A check that names its own target becomes a verifier; a verifier fired on candid output and quoted verbatim becomes proof.

Then comes the step that separates a verifier you trust from one you merely own: fire it on the candid artefact, not the polished summary. RLHF trains models to frame partial failures as partial successes, so the agent's own write-up will read smoother than the work deserves. The fix is mechanical and unsentimental — point the verifier at the run's real output, and read its words verbatim where it fired. If the summary glows, make it quote the verifier and list what didn't ship. The artefact rules; self-reports don't. A verifier only earns its authority against evidence that hasn't been rounded up.

And one restraint worth naming, because it's where good engineers over-build: this run's verifier targets one failure shape. Not a five-shape suit of armour that tries to catch everything — gold-plating catches nothing well and never ships. Cover the one mode that dominated this run. Let the kit expand later, one earned shape at a time. A verifier that fires green on a run you know was broken isn't thorough; it's mis-shaped, and the honest move is to re-scope it, not to admire its coverage.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

The test isn't "does it run." It's "does it catch what I diagnosed."

Here's the practitioner's tell that a verifier is fitted, not bolted on: point it at the un-packaged run that surfaced the failure and watch it catch what you named. A verifier that fires green on a run you know was broken is mis-shaped — and there's a sharp diagnostic for it. If your verification step runs suspiciously short, that's the smell of a too-thin check; ask whether it actually fires on the failure shape it's targeting, and if not, re-scope. The second tell is conversational: you can say in one sentence why a judge, not a hook, was right here — or why a re-feed, not a judge. If you can't articulate the fit, you guessed the shape. The verifier you fit by hand today is the precedent the next agent reads when the team encodes a shared eval, so getting its fit right once pays forward into everything built on it.

Use when

You've read a failed long-run, named where it went wrong — and you're tempted to bolt on whatever check is easiest, not the one that fits. After the refactor that compiled but drifted; the cutover that "passed" but broke an unasserted contract.

Build

One verifier file at a stable path — verifier.sh, evals/judge.md, or a stop-hook config — whose first lines name the failure it targets and the shape that catches it. Your first eval.

Test

Point it at the broken run — it catches what you diagnosed.

Why
The principle

A verifier's strength isn't its rigour — it's its fit, and its firing on candid evidence.

Your verifier is your first eval — and it only protects you if its shape matches the failure that actually happened.

The counterintuitive part: a more rigorous check is not a safer one. A meticulous shell-hook is blind to a qualitative drift; a careful judge is noise on a one-line lint's job. The eval that protects you is the one whose shape matches the failure that actually happened — read from your own artefact, not picked from a menu in the abstract. This is the verification arm of the three-pattern, re-discovered from your run rather than memorised as a taxonomy.

And it composes upward. The verifier you fit by hand today is the precedent the next agent reads when the team encodes a shared eval — and a shared CI eval is only ever a hand-fitted verifier that earned its way into the kit. That's the whole chain: one engineer's quality bar becomes a named, readable instrument, which becomes the team's eval, which becomes the company's reliability standard — built up from candid evidence, never handed down as a mandate.
