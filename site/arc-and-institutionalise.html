Arc the loop — read your own artefacts

The principle: the loop closes when the lesson ships — narrated from evidence, pruned to what still fires.

After a stretch of real work, point a fresh agent at everything you've accumulated — rules, ADRs, skills, run-notes, session logs — make it narrate the arc from the evidence, not your memory, then cut the rules the arc just proved stale. The system that ships ends up leaner, and legible to whoever inherits it.

The remembered arc is the press release. The evidence-narrated arc is the truth — and it ships leaner.
The mechanism · two narrators, one arc

Your memory is the one source that lies to flatter you.

Here is the uncomfortable part: when you describe what an arc of work taught you, the least reliable narrator in the room is you — precisely because you lived it. Memory compresses toward the flattering story. It keeps the wins, quietly edits out the dead ends, and assumes the rules you wrote weeks ago still fire when half of them no longer match how you work. The intuition behind this move is simple: recalled arcs drift toward the press release. That isn't a measured rate — it's the cognitive reason the discipline exists. The fix isn't more honesty on your part. It's reading a source that has no incentive to look good.

Across any real stretch of work you accumulate durable artefacts almost without noticing — a ./CLAUDE.local.md that grew rule by rule, a couple of ADRs written under pressure, two or three skills in ~/.claude/skills/, the session transcripts, a run's RUN-NOTES.md and its verifiers. Each was written in the moment, for the moment, with zero incentive to look good later — which is exactly what makes them the honest record. Nobody has ever read them as a set, cold. The discipline has a precise shape, and the shape is what keeps it honest. Don't narrate from the chair you've been sitting in. Open a fresh session — an agent with no memory of the arc — and hand it the artefacts explicitly: the root rules file, ./CLAUDE.local.md, the memory folder, any ADRs, every skill you authored there, the run artefacts. Bounded, named, on disk. Not "read the whole history" — a specific read-list. A fresh agent reading named files can't flatter you, because it never lived the wins; that's the source's rationale for the move, not a guarantee that any agent reads objectively.

The artefacts are the truth; your memory is the press release — so read the truth, narrated by something that never lived the wins.

Then make it earn every line. Ask for the through-line the evidence shows — what changed across the arc, in what order, what each artefact reveals you learned — and require it to quote your own files back at you rather than summarise. The arc you remember and the arc your CLAUDE.local.md git log actually traces are two different stories, and the second one is true. The instruction that does the work is blunt: write from my artefacts, quote me where you can, skip the summary of what the training taught. If the agent can't point to the line each claim came from, it narrated from your chair — re-run with quote-required.

The upgrade move · head-only → evidence-narrated + pruned

From the arc in your head to a note the next reader inherits.

The default failure mode here has a tell. The agent writes the arc note in the voice of a glossy training recap — "this journey taught me to embrace…" — and it reads like a testimonial. Catch it and push back, because the artefacts don't talk like that. A retrospective that sounds like marketing copy is narrating your memory, not your evidence. The negative test is sharp: if a stranger could mistake the note for marketing copy, the voice slipped — send it back to the files. What you want reads like an engineer's field note, not a brochure. The pruning arm is the same mechanic pointed inward, and it's where most people quietly cheat. Reading the kit cold exposes rules that no longer fire, no longer match how you work, or were superseded by a later one. Cut them — in place, with the deletion visible in git history. A rules file that only ever grows is a file nobody re-reads, because the cost of reading it climbs with every addition until everyone skims. The source names the failure exactly: addition-only compounding — the student who only adds rules and never subtracts. An instrument calibrates by removing drift as much as by adding range. The arc-read is the moment you earn the right to shrink the kit; the system that ships should be leaner than the system that accumulated.

And then it has to land somewhere it'll actually be read. The one-page note isn't a journal entry — it's the cold-start brief for whoever inherits the repo, whether that's a teammate or future-you six weeks on. Ship it where a new session looks first: an ADR, an observations/ memo, or a standalone file you'll point an agent at. The loop only closes when the lesson ships somewhere it'll be re-read — narrated from evidence, pruned to what still fires.

The compounding loop · what changes each lap

Each lap the kit gets fuller and leaner, and the next reader starts cheaper.

The picture below closes the thread through the soil. An arc of real work runs; artefacts accumulate; a fresh agent reads them cold and narrates the through-line; you cut the rules the arc proved stale; the one-page note ships where the next session reads first; the next arc starts on a leaner, legible base — and the lessons that accumulate next are subtler, because the obvious ones are already encoded. Notice the property that matters: every lap the kit is both fuller and leaner, and the cold-start cost for the next reader drops. That's not a parlor trick. That's how a personal pile of artefacts becomes a system someone else can stand on.

What "Test" actually means · in practice

The test is whether a stranger can rebuild the arc without asking you.

Three checks tell you the arc landed, and none of them is "it reads well." First: the note quotes your own artefacts — not the training, not your memory. If you can't point to the line in CLAUDE.local.md or the ADR each claim came from, the agent narrated from your chair; re-run with quote-required. Second: it reads like an engineer's field note, not a testimonial — if a stranger could mistake it for marketing copy, send it back to the evidence. Third, and the one that's easiest to skip: the git history shows at least one rule cut, not only rules added. Pure accretion means you read the kit to admire it, not to prune it. The real test of institutionalisation: a fresh agent — or a teammate — pointed at only the note plus the kit can reconstruct what the arc taught without asking you. That's the proof the knowledge survived leaving your head.

Use when

You've finished a stretch of real work — a multi-task arc, a long-running send-off, a quarter of builds — and the only record of what you learned lives in your head, where it decays and flatters you. The kit has grown past what you can hold in your head; someone's about to inherit the repo.

Build

Two outputs from one read: a one-page arc note narrated from evidence, landed where the next session reads first — and a leaner ./CLAUDE.local.md with at least one stale rule cut in place, the deletion visible in git.

Test

A stranger pointed at the note + kit can rebuild the arc.

Why
The principle

The loop closes when the lesson ships — but only durably when it ships in a form the next reader can absorb cold.

"The artefacts are the truth; your memory is the press release — so read the truth, narrated by something that never lived the wins."

You trust your memory of what you learned, and your memory is the one source that systematically lies — it keeps the wins and quietly deletes the dead ends. The counterintuitive part: the least reliable narrator of your own arc is you, precisely because you lived it. A git log, an ADR written under pressure, a skill's description, a RUN-NOTES.md — these were written in the moment with no incentive to look good later, which is exactly what makes them the honest record. So you hand them to an agent that never lived the arc, and it narrates from what's actually there.

The subtraction arm is the same mechanic pointed inward: a rules file that only grows is a file nobody re-reads, because the cost of reading it climbs with every addition until everyone skims. And the institutionalisation matters because a learning system whose state lives only in one practitioner's head has a learning rate that resets to zero the day that person leaves the repo. Narrate from evidence, prune to what still fires — that's how a personal pile of artefacts becomes a system the next person can stand on.

Your memory is the press release. The artefacts are the truth.
