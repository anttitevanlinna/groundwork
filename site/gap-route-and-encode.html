Sort each lesson to the clock where it fires

Assume you will get it wrong. The failure is the learning. OODA. Observe, orient, decide, act.

The principle: the loop closes when the lesson ships — but only to the clock where it'll act.

After a run you hold a fistful of lessons, and the mistake is to treat them as one bin — call the bin a skill and pour everything in. Each lesson has a clock: one acts before the run, one during it, one on the next run of this shape. Route a lesson to the wrong clock and it goes mute at exactly the moment you needed it. A private fifteen-minute pass — but also the dispatcher a company-scale context layer either institutionalises or drowns under: prevention in memory, detection in CI, reuse in skills, instead of one undifferentiated pile.

Three windows, three clocks. A skill is a repeat prescription — useless for the acute mistake you should have prevented upstream.
The dispensary image · why one counter is the wrong shape

A dispensary is three windows, not one counter.

Walk into a hospital dispensary and you do not meet a single till that does everything. You meet three windows, and each one runs on a different clock. Window one is pre-packed: the pharmacist loads the blister pack before the patient ever arrives — the drug acts before the symptom shows, the decision already made upstream, nothing to decide in the moment. Window two is stat: the dispense happens while the patient is in front of you — a check, a dose, an intervention that would be useless either earlier or later, because it only works live. Window three is the repeat prescription: the pharmacist writes it out for the next presentation of this condition — not this patient, not right now — a template that travels.

Hold that picture, because it is the whole card. After a run you are standing at the counter with a fistful of lessons, and the reflex is to shove all of them through one window labelled skill. But a lesson poured into the wrong window either spoils on the shelf or arrives after the damage is done. The three windows here — a rule in memory, a sharper check, a new skill — are the source curriculum's three-way sort, drawn from a convergent practitioner pattern read off the encode end of Armin Ronacher's reference / plan.md / verifier trio. One honest caveat that travels with every recurrence below: it's that source's sort, a useful and well-worn one — not an official Claude Code taxonomy and not a measured law. Stay open to a fourth window. What is observed is plainer than any taxonomy: a lesson filed where its clock never strikes is a lesson that goes mute.

Use when · the moment you reach for the bundle

You're holding a short stack, and the reflex is to call it a skill.

You have just compared two runs of the same task — once un-packaged, once packaged — or post-mortemed a single bad one, and you are holding a short stack of realisations: something the agent should never have done; something that should have set off an alarm halfway through; something you want available next time this shape of task appears. The reflex is to bundle all three into a skill and call it a ship. That reflex is wrong — not because skills are bad, but because a skill only catches the next presentation. It does nothing to prevent the same mistake in this type of run, and it cannot interrupt a run already in progress. Bundling is comfortable; routing is the actual work.

This recurs at every loop closure: after any two-run compare, after a CI red that surfaces a mistake the loop should have caught, at the end of any session that produced a surprise you want the next session to inherit. The source names the trap outright — call it gap-home confusion, the practitioner observation that engineers pack everything into the second skill because skills feel like the answer. (It's a teaching note about why loops stall, not a measured rate.) Most engineers either skip the routing — everything stays in their head — or collapse it, where everything becomes a skill. Both leave the lesson mute.

The three windows · what each one is, and what it cannot do

Each window holds one kind of lesson — and refuses the others.

PREVENT — acts before the run. A rule the model reads on every invocation. Its home is a personal rules file: ./CLAUDE.local.md (gitignored, per-project) or a global equivalent. Rules are blister-packed: loaded before the session opens, invisible during it, doing their work in the negative space of things that simply do not happen. If you diagnosed a gap and a single sentence in a rules file would have closed it upstream — a habit the agent should have had from cold start — that is where it lives. Not in a skill that only fires when someone invokes it. The shape of the lesson is "the agent should never have done X."

DETECT — acts during the run. A check that fires mid-execution. Not a reminder to the agent — an actual gate: a verifier, a lint, a structural assertion, a self-critique step the agent runs before proceeding. Window two, stat. It is useless pre-packed (what would it check before anything has happened?) and useless after the fact (the damage is done). Rules prevent; they do not catch. If the diagnosis is "we never knew it had gone wrong until the very end," the lesson belongs here, in a sharpened or newly-fitted verifier — not a rule and not a skill.

REUSE — acts on the next run of this shape. A skill: a procedural template the agent invokes when it recognises this category of task again. Window three, the repeat prescription. It travels — that is its virtue. But it only fires when invoked, so it cannot prevent and it cannot interrupt. If the lesson is "I keep re-explaining this sequence from scratch," that is a skill — its home is the repeat-prescription window, and the routing stops there (how to forge it well is the next card's job, not this one's). Do not conflate a rule with a skill because the word "skill" feels satisfying to ship.

A rule that prevents a mistake upstream does more, cheaper work than a skill that re-catches it every run.

Subtraction is part of the sort · expiry is real

The same pass that stocks a packet should pull an expired one.

Routing is not only addition. The two-run contrast often shows a rule in ./CLAUDE.local.md that didn't fire, didn't help, or no longer matches how you work. The same diagnostic pass that adds a rule should ask: is there a rule already here that this new one supersedes? Cut it, visibly. A rules file that only ever grows has stopped being read honestly — it is a filing cabinet nobody opens, and a cabinet full of outdated prescriptions is not a safety net, it is noise that trains the reader, human or model, to skim. A gap can be negative: the absence the loop wants is a rule removed, not added. The source calls this compound-by-subtraction, and names the opposite as a failure mode — addition-only compounding, where you only ever add rules and never cut one.

This is the part most practitioners skip. They add the new rule, feel the satisfaction, and leave the superseded one in place "just in case." Just-in-case rules are the expired blister packs stacked at the back of the shelf. Expiry is real in a dispensary; it should be real in your rules file. The git history is your audit trail — make the deletion visible there, not just locally. The routing decision · ask this of each lesson

Three questions. Whichever answers first names the window.

Don't reach for the fix on the first lesson. Lay the stack out flat — three, maybe four, dominant first — so you can route them as a set, not one impulsive skill at a time. The source caps this deliberately: two runs, maybe three gaps each; pick the three that cost the most. Over-routing is its own failure; a long catalogue produces noise, not a loop. Then ask each lesson, in order:

1 · Would a rule have prevented this upstream, before the run opened? → ./CLAUDE.local.md or equivalent. Write the rule in the imperative; keep it short enough to be read, not skimmed. 2 · Would a check have caught this mid-run, while the agent still had room to correct? → tighten the verifier, add the assertion, sharpen the self-critique step. The check lives in the agent's task protocol, not in a background file. 3 · Is this a move I will need to re-perform next time this shape appears, explained from scratch every time without it? → that is a skill. One of three destinations; not the default.

If a lesson fits none cleanly, it probably wants to be a note — a post-mortem line, not a standing instruction. Notes are not windows; they are the pharmacist's handwriting on the back of the packet — useful once, not refiled as standing orders. And remember the caveat that travels with this whole sort: this taxonomy is a practitioner sort, not an official architecture. There may be a fourth window you discover. The point is not the categories; it is the habit of asking when does this need to fire? before you write anything down.

Three clocks a lesson can strike. Send it to the wrong clock and it never strikes at all — which is why the satisfying skill bundle is the wrong default.

Monday move: pick the last run that surprised you. Write the one lesson from it as a sentence. Before you file it anywhere, ask: when would this sentence have needed to fire to change the outcome? If "before the run opened," open ./CLAUDE.local.md right now and write the rule. While you're there, read the last three rules and delete the stale one. Do that tonight; the habit compounds from the first repetition. ./CLAUDE.local.md gitignored · verifier is your own repo · skill ships to ~/.claude/skills/ · ~15 min.

What you end up with · and how you know it worked

After one diagnostic pass: a rules file with one addition and one visible deletion; a tighter verifier or a new assertion in the task protocol; possibly a skill entry for a recurring move. Three short artefacts routed to three different clocks. Each one will strike where it is needed; none will be mute.

Run the same task shape a week later and read the windows. The rule-shaped lesson does not require mention — the agent simply does not make the upstream mistake. The check-shaped lesson fires mid-run and you see it catch something. The skill-shaped lesson surfaces when the agent recognises the pattern and reaches for the template. And the structural tells: each lesson has exactly one named window, and you can say in one sentence why that window and not the other two. At least one lesson routed to memory as a deletion, the git history showing a rule pulled — pure accretion means you skipped subtraction. And not everything became a skill.

The negative test catches the most common slip: if the skill you authored reads like a documentation page describing the gap rather than moving to catch it — its description ending with a topic, not an outcome — it's mis-routed; that lesson was probably a rule. (Skill-as-documentation is the source's named diagnostic, a practitioner teaching observation about why loops stall — not a measured rate.) If you ever find yourself re-explaining something you thought you had sorted, a lesson is in the wrong window. Re-sort.

Why
The principle

The loop closes when the lesson ships — but only to the clock where it'll act.

A skill is a repeat prescription — brilliant for chronic conditions, useless for the acute mistake you should have prevented upstream.

The consulting instinct — and the AI-tooling instinct — is to make everything a skill, because skills are the exciting artefact. They have names, they feel shipped, they feel like capability. But a skill is a repeat prescription, and a repeat prescription for an acute, preventable condition is not medicine — it is a protocol that arrives after the damage. That is precisely why undisciplined engineers route everything to the heaviest window.

The real question is temporal: at what moment in the causal chain does this lesson need to act? Rules act early and cheaply, before anything has gone wrong. Checks act at the decision point, when correction is still possible. Skills act on recurrence, when the shape repeats. Route each to the moment it is actually useful and your personal operating system starts to behave like infrastructure — silent, load-bearing, trustworthy — rather than a pile of good intentions. The compounding is asymmetric: a rule that prevents a mistake compounds silently (you never pay the tax), while a skill that arrives a lesson too late costs every time the shape recurs. Triage is not a metaphor for tidiness; it is a leverage decision.
